The whole field on one page

Every idea in the course fits on the map below. The center is the thing we keep coming back to: an eval run, in which each task is attempted several times (trials) by a system in an environment, each attempt leaves a transcript, and a fixed grader scores it. Everything else either feeds that run, scores it, interprets it, or threatens it. Read the arrows as sentences: "Datasets feed samples into an eval run", "Pitfalls can invalidate decisions".

Three things about this map are worth saying out loud, because experts use them constantly:

  • The grader is an instrument with its own error (Module 03, Module 13). Every arrow out of "Graders" carries that error forward, so a metric can never be more trustworthy than the grader that produced it.
  • Benchmarks are just datasets plus graders that someone froze and published. That is why they standardize comparisons, and also why they leak into training data and saturate (Module 18).
  • Safety evals are evals with a governance action attached. Their mechanics are the same, but the question flips from "how good is it?" to "can we rule out that it is this dangerous?" (Module 17).

The benchmark atlas

There are thousands of benchmarks. About forty of them come up again and again in papers, system cards and interviews, and the Part V and VI modules add about thirty more (listed after the first table below). The atlas places them on two axes. Horizontally: the modality or kind of agent they test. Vertically: the horizon, meaning roughly how long and how many steps a task takes, which correlates strongly with difficulty for today's agents. Dashed outlines mark benchmarks that no longer separate frontier systems, because they are saturated, contaminated or dated.

How to read the atlas like an expert:

  • The frontier moves up and to the right. Knowledge benchmarks (MMLU, then GPQA) saturated first; the field replaced them with HLE and with agentic, long-horizon work (METR time horizon, GDPval, SWE-bench Pro, MLE-bench, PaperBench, RE-Bench).
  • Every saturated benchmark has a successor with the same shape but harder or cleaner data: HumanEval → HumanEval+ and LiveCodeBench; SWE-bench Verified → SWE-bench Pro and SWE-rebench; ScreenSpot → ScreenSpot-Pro; OSWorld → OSWorld-Verified; τ-bench → τ²-bench (and the τ³ release); ARC-AGI-1 → ARC-AGI-2 → the interactive ARC-AGI-3.
  • Voice sits low on the atlas for a reason. τ-Voice (March 2026) found voice agents keep only about 30 to 45% of their text-mode capability on the same grounded tasks (31 to 51% clean vs 85% for a text reasoning model), so the field is still measuring basic task completion in speech (Module 09).

A frame for answering anything

Hard eval questions look different on the surface ("design an eval for X", "is this result real?", "why do users hate the new version?") but they have the same skeleton. Experts walk through the same six steps every time, out loud, in order. If you do this in an interview you will sound senior even before you reach the clever part, because you are showing that you know where evals go wrong.

  1. Decision. What will you do differently depending on the result? Ship or not, which model, whether a release needs extra safeguards. This fixes what "good enough" means.
  2. System. Name the unit under test: model, prompt, scaffold, tools, retrieval, budget (steps, tokens, time). Anything you do not pin is a confound.
  3. Data. Where tasks come from (real traffic and real failures first), how they cover the risky cases, how many you need (power analysis), and how you keep a clean held-out split.
  4. Grader. Code if you can (state checks, tests), a model judge where you must (validated against human labels, reporting TPR and TNR), humans for calibration and for the highest-stakes calls.
  5. Number. The metric that matches the decision, with a 95% confidence interval, the number of trials per task, and cost and latency next to quality.
  6. Threats. Contamination, noise, harness differences, judge bias, flaky environments, reward hacking, evaluation awareness. Say which you checked and how.

Visual cheat sheets

Most expert questions reduce to eight recurring choices: which grader, which metric, how to read pass@k against pass^k, how big the noise is, what to do when a number moves, how to evaluate one building block of an agent, how to report a security result, and how high the bar is when a regulator is the audience. Each cheat sheet below is one of those choices drawn as a picture. Learn them well enough to sketch on a whiteboard, and you will have a ready answer for most "how would you measure X?" questions.

Which grader should I use?

Ask three questions in order and stop at the first "yes". The order matters: code graders are the cheapest and most repeatable, so you only move down the ladder when code genuinely cannot see what you need (Module 03). Whatever you pick, humans stay in the loop as the source of labels that keep the automatic graders honest.

Which metric answers my question?

A metric is only "right" relative to a decision. The same transcripts can be summarized as pass@k, pass^k, a mean score or a win rate, and each tells a different story (Module 04). Start from the question on the left and read across.

How big is the noise?

Before arguing about any score difference, know the size of the error bar. For a success rate the 95% half-width is 1.96 × √(p(1 − p)/n), which is largest at p = 0.5. The curve below plots that worst case and marks four well-known benchmark sizes. The shape is the lesson: every halving of the error bar costs four times the tasks (Module 04).

"My eval score moved. Why?"

This is the everyday debugging question, and the expert answer is an ordered checklist, cheapest check first. Most "regressions" and "breakthroughs" die at step 1 or 2. Only after ruling out noise, measurement changes and infrastructure do you get to the interesting part: which tasks flipped and what the transcripts show (Module 12, Module 15).

Evaluating a building block: isolate, ablate, attribute

Part V applies one method to four different parts of an agent. Change one block, hold everything else fixed, compare paired runs, then read the tasks that flipped. What changes from block to block is the signature metric, the number that tells you the block is doing its job.

Security eval: attack success against utility

A security number means nothing on its own. An agent that refuses every task has an attack success rate of zero and is useless; a defense that stops yesterday's attacks can fall to an attacker who adapts. Plot both numbers, and say who the attacker was.

Regulator-facing evidence against engineering evidence

The same eval runs can serve a product decision and a regulator, but the bar is different. Five things change when the audience changes, and a safety case is how the evidence is organized once it has to convince someone else.

Foundations

1. What exactly is an eval, and how is it different from a benchmark and from a unit test?

An eval is a fixed set of tasks, run through a specific system, scored by a grader and aggregated into metrics, so that versions of the system can be compared fairly. A benchmark is an eval that has been frozen and published so that many parties can compare on it; that public, static nature is exactly what makes it prone to contamination and saturation. A unit test asserts one deterministic correct output; an eval estimates a rate over a distribution of outputs, many of which are acceptable, so it needs repeats and error bars. The grader in an eval also has an error rate, which a unit test's assert does not. See Module 01.

2. Capability evals vs regression evals: what pass rate should each have, and why?

Anthropic's January 2026 agent-evals post puts it cleanly: capability evals should start at a low pass rate, because their job is to show headroom and measure progress on things the system cannot yet do. Regression evals should sit near 100%, because they guard behavior you already have; any drop is a signal. Mixing them hides both signals: a suite averaging 80% can hide a regression on a critical flow under a capability gain elsewhere. Hamel Husain adds a practical heuristic for product evals: if everything passes, the eval probably is not hard enough; he suggests around 70% is healthier for an improvement suite. See Module 02.

3. Why is "the model" the wrong unit of evaluation for agents?

Because agent scores belong to the combination of model × scaffold/harness × tools × budget. The same model can move many points when you change the harness: mini-SWE-agent reached 65% on SWE-bench Verified with about 100 lines of Python, which is why swebench.com runs a "bash-only" view with every model in the same minimal harness. OSWorld results depend on the step budget (15, 50 or 100 steps), and Terminal-Bench reports model × agent-harness pairs. So always state the harness, the budget and the number of attempts, and only compare under the same ones. See Module 05 and Module 07.

4. Outcome grading vs process (trajectory) grading: when do you use each?

Outcome grading checks the final state of the environment (the database row, the file, the passing tests). It is robust to the many valid paths an agent can take, which is why τ-bench, OSWorld, AppWorld and SWE-bench grade outcomes. Process grading scores the trajectory: which tools were called, with what arguments, in what order, and whether any step was unsafe. Use it when the path itself matters (a forbidden action, a policy step such as identity verification, wasted cost) or to diagnose failures. Rigid trajectory matching (for example ADK's default EXACT tool_trajectory_avg_score of 1.0) punishes valid alternatives, so prefer in-order or any-order matching, or a rubric judge over the transcript. A strong setup grades outcomes for the headline metric and processes for safety and diagnosis. See Module 05.

5. You are starting a new agent product with no eval data. What do you do in week one? design

Collect real traces first: dogfood the agent and log around 100 full transcripts. Do open coding (a free-text note on the first failure in each trace), then axial coding (group the notes into a failure taxonomy and count). Turn the most frequent and most costly failure modes into 20 to 50 tasks with unambiguous success criteria and reference solutions, which is the starting size Anthropic recommends. Grade with code where possible, write a narrow binary judge only for failures that survive prompt fixes, and wire the suite into CI as a regression gate. Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar report spending 60 to 80% of development time on error analysis and evaluation; that is the right order of magnitude. See Module 12.

Statistics

6. Compare pass@k and pass^k. When does each matter?

pass@k is the probability that at least one of k attempts succeeds; pass^k is the probability that all k succeed. If each trial succeeds independently with probability p, pass@k = 1 − (1 − p)^k and pass^k = p^k. With p = 0.8 and k = 5, pass@5 ≈ 99.97% but pass^5 ≈ 32.8%: the same agent looks nearly perfect or unreliable depending on the question. From n samples with c correct, the unbiased estimators are pass@k = 1 − C(n−c, k)/C(n, k) (Chen et al. 2021) and pass^k = C(c, k)/C(n, k) (Yao et al. 2024). Use pass@k when something downstream picks the best attempt (a test suite, a human reviewer, best-of-n with a verifier); use pass^k for autonomous, customer-facing agents that run the same kind of task thousands of times. The τ-bench paper reported that even a strong function-calling agent (gpt-4o) fell below 25% pass^8 in its retail domain. See Module 04.

7. On 200 tasks, model B beats model A by 3 points. Is it real?

Probably not yet. The standard error of a single score near 70% on n = 200 is √(0.7 × 0.3 / 200) ≈ 3.2 points, so each score alone has a 95% interval of about ±6.4 points. But A and B ran on the same tasks, so use a paired test: only the discordant tasks carry information. Suppose A alone solved 10 and B alone solved 16. The difference is 6/200 = 3 points, with standard error √(26 − 6²/200)/200 ≈ 2.5 points, giving z ≈ 1.2: not significant. The fixes are more tasks, more trials per task (which reduces within-task variance), and a power analysis before running. Anthropic's "Adding Error Bars to Evals" (Miller, 2024) is the standard reference. See Module 04.

8. What are clustered standard errors, and when do you need them?

When tasks are not independent (several questions about the same passage, several instances from the same repo, several variants of one task template), treating them as independent overstates your effective sample size. Clustered standard errors compute variance at the level of the cluster instead. Miller (2024) reports that naive standard errors can understate uncertainty by more than 3x in realistic eval sets. In agent evals, the usual clusters are task templates (WebArena's 812 tasks come from 241 templates), repositories (SWE-bench is dominated by a few repos) and simulated user personas. See Module 04.

9. How many tasks do you need?

Two rules of thumb and one formula. For a single score near 50%, the 95% half-width is about 1.96 × √(0.25/n): ±9.8 points at n = 100, ±4.9 at 400, ±2.5 at 1,600. Quadrupling the data only halves the interval. For comparing two systems with a paired test, n ≈ ((zα/2 + zpower) × SD(d) / δ)², with z = 1.96 and 0.84 for 95% confidence and 80% power. If about 13% of tasks are discordant, SD(d) ≈ 0.36, and detecting a 3-point difference needs n ≈ (2.8 × 0.36 / 0.03)² ≈ 1,130 tasks. For agent evals where tasks are expensive, add trials per task instead of tasks and use pass^k; for quick product iteration, start with 20 to 50 tasks and look for large effects. See Module 04.

10. Where does variance in agent evals come from, and how do you reduce it?

Sampling temperature and reasoning randomness; compounding over many steps (a small divergence early leads to a different trajectory); the environment itself (network, timeouts, app versions, live websites); the simulated user in conversational evals (τ-bench notes the simulator can hallucinate or end early); and judge noise. Reduce it by pinning versions and container images, resetting state between trials, replacing live dependencies with stable mocks (StableToolBench did this for ToolBench), running several trials per task and reporting mean ± SE or pass^k, and using paired comparisons. Separate flaky infrastructure failures from agent failures in the logs, or your error bars measure your DevOps. See Module 04 and Module 05.

11. Your judge's pass rate is 80%, but the judge is imperfect. What is the real pass rate?

Measure the judge against human labels to get its true positive rate (TPR, how often it passes truly good outputs) and true negative rate (TNR, how often it fails truly bad ones). Then correct with θ = (pobs + TNR − 1) / (TPR + TNR − 1). With pobs = 0.80, TPR = 0.90 and TNR = 0.80, θ = 0.60 / 0.70 ≈ 0.857. The correction becomes unstable as TPR + TNR approaches 1 (a judge no better than chance), and the uncertainty in TPR and TNR should be propagated, for example with a bootstrap over the labeled set. See Module 13; the widget below does the arithmetic.

12. How do Elo and Bradley–Terry ratings work, and what does a 100-point gap mean?

Both model the chance that A is preferred to B as a function of the rating gap only: P(A wins) = 1 / (1 + 10^(−(RA − RB)/400)). A 100-point gap means about a 64% win rate; 200 points about 76%. Chatbot Arena started with online Elo and moved to a Bradley–Terry model fitted by maximum likelihood with bootstrap confidence intervals, plus "style control" to adjust for length and markdown. The known weaknesses: preference is not capability (style and sycophancy win votes), and "The Leaderboard Illusion" (2025) showed private testing of many variants with best-of-N publishing and unequal access to battle data. See Module 04 and Module 18.

Graders and judges

13. What is wrong with BLEU (and ROUGE) for agents?

BLEU counts n-gram overlap with a reference text. That fails agents in three ways. First, agents are judged on actions and state, not prose: whether the refund was actually issued is in the database, not in the words. Second, overlap rewards surface similarity: "Refund not issued for order 123" overlaps heavily with "Refund issued for order 123" but means the opposite, while "I've processed your refund" overlaps little but is correct. Third, there are many valid outputs and paths, and a single reference cannot cover them. Use state checks, execution-based tests, AST matching for tool calls, and validated model judges for open text; keep BLEU-family metrics for narrow tasks such as translation, where even there chrF and COMET are preferred. OpenAI's (now deprecated) graders offered BLEU and ROUGE, which is a reminder that "available" is not "appropriate". See Module 03.

14. Your judge says quality went up 5% but users complain. What do you do? design

Treat it as two instruments disagreeing and find out which one is wrong. (1) Is the +5% real? Run a paired test on the same items with enough trials; it may be noise. (2) Is the judge still right? Hand-label 100 to 200 fresh outputs from the new version and recompute TPR and TNR: judges drift when the output distribution shifts, and known biases (length, position, self-preference) can reward the new version's style. (3) Is the data still right? Compare the eval set with current traffic; the complaints may come from intents the set does not cover. (4) Is the rubric measuring what users care about? Code the complaints (open and axial coding) and check whether the rubric even mentions those failure modes (tone, latency, wrong action). Then fix the weakest link, add production outcomes (task completion, escalations, complaint rate) beside the judge, and gate releases on both. See the decision tree below and Module 13.

15. How do you validate an LLM judge before trusting it?

Pick one principal domain expert and have them label a diverse set with a binary pass/fail plus a written critique (Hamel Husain's "critique shadowing"). Split the labels into train (few-shot examples), dev (iterate on the prompt) and test (report once). Report TPR and TNR on the test split, not raw accuracy, because class imbalance hides failures; for subjective tasks also report Cohen's kappa against human agreement. Keep each judge narrow, one failure mode per judge; the most common mistake is asking one judge to catch many kinds of error at once. Re-validate whenever the system or the judge model changes. As a reference point, the MT-Bench paper found a strong judge agreed with humans over 80% of the time, about the same as humans agree with each other. See Module 13.

16. Name the main judge biases and how to mitigate each.

Position bias: preferring the first (or second) answer in a pairwise comparison; mitigate by swapping order and averaging, or counting only consistent verdicts. Verbosity bias: preferring longer answers; mitigate with rubrics that penalize padding, length-controlled comparisons, and style control. Self-enhancement bias: preferring outputs from the judge's own model family; mitigate by using a different model to grade than to generate (Anthropic's docs recommend this). Limited reasoning: judges miss maths and logic errors; mitigate with reference-guided grading and letting the judge reason before scoring. And scale compression: 1 to 10 scores cluster; prefer binary or labeled categories. All five are documented in Zheng et al. 2023 and practitioner guides by Eugene Yan and Hamel Husain. See Module 13.

17. When do you use human graders, and how do you make them reliable?

Use humans to create ground truth for calibrating code and model graders, for high-stakes or subjective judgments (medical, legal, taste), and for final checks before launch. Make them reliable with a written rubric with examples at each level, a calibration round, overlapping assignments so you can measure inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's or Fleiss' kappa), and a "benevolent dictator" domain expert who settles disputes. Expect criteria drift: Shankar et al. (2024) showed people refine their criteria while grading, so budget for a second pass. Anthropic's docs rank humans as the most flexible but slowest grading method, to be avoided where a validated automatic grader will do. GDPval is the large-scale example: blinded expert pairwise comparisons across 44 occupations. See Module 03.

Agent evals, modality by modality

18. Design an eval for a voice agent that books doctor appointments. design

Decision: can it go live on the clinic line, and for which call types? System: telephony audio → ASR → LLM with calendar and patient-record tools → TTS, or a speech-to-speech model; pin every component version. Data: a simulated-caller matrix of intents (book, reschedule, cancel, urgent symptoms, wrong number) crossed with accents, noise, speaking speed and interruption behavior, plus replays of consented real calls; Cekura-style persona matrices easily reach hundreds of variants. Graders, in layers: component (WER = (S + D + I)/N on names, dates and dates of birth; latency P50/P95, where vendor guides target sub-second time to first audio), conversation (turn-taking, barge-in handling, reading back the date and time before confirming), task outcome checked in the database (right patient, right doctor, right slot, no double booking), and safety (never diagnoses, routes urgent symptoms to a human or emergency line, verifies identity before disclosing anything). Number: task success as pass^k per scenario, because every caller must succeed, plus latency percentiles and escalation rate. Threats: simulator unrealism (calibrate against real calls), transcribe-then-judge missing audio problems, and noisy-channel robustness (τ-Voice saw scores drop further with noise and accents). 19. How would you tell if a SWE-bench gain is real? design

Ask six questions. (1) Same harness? Compare only in the same scaffold and budget, ideally the bash-only mini-SWE-agent view on swebench.com; a new scaffold can be worth more than a new model. (2) Same number of attempts? pass@1 vs best-of-n with a selector are different metrics. (3) Is it outside noise? On 500 tasks a score near 50% has a standard error of about 2.2 points; run several trials and a paired per-instance test. (4) Is it contamination? In February 2026 OpenAI reported that every frontier model it tested could reproduce gold patches verbatim, and that at least 59.4% of audited problems had flawed tests; OpenAI stopped reporting Verified and recommends SWE-bench Pro. Check on fresh or private tasks (SWE-bench Pro's held-out and commercial splits, SWE-rebench). (5) Where did it come from? Break down by repo (Django is a large share) and by task difficulty. (6) Is it honest? Read transcripts for test tampering or special-casing. A gain that survives all six is real. See Module 07 and Module 18.

20. How do you evaluate a computer-use agent safely? design

Isolation first: run every trial in a disposable VM or container that is reset from a snapshot (OSWorld uses VMs; Inspect supports Docker, Kubernetes and other sandboxes), with synthetic accounts, no real credentials, and network egress restricted to an allowlist. Grade outcomes with state checks on files and apps, and also check for collateral damage: nothing changed that should not have (AppWorld's state-based tests do this). Include infeasible tasks where the right behavior is to stop and say so (OSWorld has them), prompt-injection pages and documents planted in the environment, and irreversible actions (delete, pay, send) that should trigger a confirmation request. Report unsafe-action rate and injection success rate alongside task success, cap steps and wall-clock time, and log full screenshots and actions for review. Only after that move to supervised runs in a staging copy of real systems. See Module 08.

21. How do you evaluate a multi-agent system? design

Start with the question most teams skip: does it beat a single-agent baseline at equal cost? Anthropic's research system (June 2025) beat single-agent Claude Opus 4 by 90.2% on an internal research eval, but multi-agent runs used about 15x the tokens of a chat turn, and on BrowseComp token usage alone explained 80% of performance variance. Then grade the end state with a rubric (they used factual accuracy, citation accuracy, completeness, source quality and tool efficiency), because runs take different valid paths. Add process metrics from the trace tree: handoff accuracy (OpenAI's guide names it for multi-agent systems), duplicated work, subagent count vs query complexity, and termination. Label failures with the MAST taxonomy (system design issues, inter-agent misalignment, task verification). Keep humans in the loop: Anthropic's testers noticed agents preferring SEO content farms over authoritative sources, which the judge missed. See Module 11.

22. How would you evaluate a customer-support agent that uses tools under a policy?

Build a τ-bench-style environment: mock APIs over a database, a policy document, and an LLM-simulated user with a hidden goal. Grade by comparing the final database state with the annotated goal state, plus checks that required information was communicated. Report pass^k, because the business cares that the agent succeeds every time the same request arrives; τ-bench's own results showed pass^k falling steeply with k. Add policy-violation checks on the trajectory (acting before verifying identity, refunds above a limit). Control simulator noise by validating a sample of simulated conversations by hand, and fix ambiguous tasks: the τ² repository lists 75+ task fixes. τ²-bench's "dual-control" telecom domain adds the case where the user must also act on their own device. See Module 06.

23. How do you evaluate a RAG system?

Separate retrieval from generation, because they fail differently. Retrieval: context precision and recall against labeled relevant passages (or recall@k), measured on real queries. Generation: faithfulness (every claim supported by the retrieved context), answer relevance, and answer correctness against a reference. TruLens calls groundedness, context relevance and answer relevance the "RAG triad"; Ragas and DeepEval ship similar metrics, mostly judge-based, so validate them on your domain. Include unanswerable questions (the right answer is "I don't know") and questions whose answer changed over time. When scores drop, the split tells you whether to fix the index or the prompt. See Module 06.

24. How do you evaluate image and video models, both understanding and generation?

For understanding, the classic trap is questions answerable without looking: MMMU-Pro filtered out text-only-solvable questions and added a vision-only setting, and MVBench has items answerable from a single frame. Always run a blind (no image) baseline. For generation, combine automated checks for things detectors can verify (GenEval uses an object detector for count, color and position; T2I-CompBench uses BLIP-VQA and UniDet; VBench splits video quality into 16 dimensions) with human pairwise preference (arenas with Bradley–Terry ratings) or preference models such as HPSv2, ImageReward and PickScore. Know the weaknesses: GenEval is saturated and can be gamed by prompt rewriting, and arena voters have style biases. See Module 10.

25. How do you evaluate long-horizon agents?

Measure length directly and give partial credit. METR's time horizon fits a logistic curve of success against the log of how long the task takes a human expert, and reports the length at which success is 50% (and 80%). It has doubled roughly every seven months since 2019, with a faster pace recently: Time Horizon 1.1 (January 2026) estimated doubling times of 196.5 days over all years and 88.6 days since 2024. Within a task, use milestones or rubric leaves (PaperBench has 8,316 gradable sub-tasks) so a near-miss is visible. Budget cost and wall-clock time explicitly. And remember compounding: 95% per-step reliability over 20 steps gives 0.95²⁰ ≈ 36% end to end. METR itself cautions that the horizon is a statistic of a software-task distribution, not "the model can work for N hours". See Module 11.

26. How would you build a coding-agent eval from your own repository? design

Mine your history the way SWE-bench mined GitHub: take merged pull requests that fixed an issue and came with tests. For each, check out the parent commit, give the agent the issue text, and grade with the tests the PR added (FAIL_TO_PASS) plus the existing tests (PASS_TO_PASS), inside a pinned container. Hide the tests from the agent, and screen tasks by hand for well-specified issues and tests that accept any correct fix (OpenAI's audit found overly strict tests were the most common flaw). Because your private code is not in any training set, this is naturally contamination-resistant, but keep a freshness window as models are retrained. Track cost, turns and whether the agent modified tests. See Module 07.

27. Walk me through reproducing a public benchmark run with Inspect.

Think in families. A startup needs tracing plus a CI regression gate with little setup: an observability platform with evals attached (Braintrust, LangSmith, or self-hosted Langfuse or Phoenix) and a code-first test runner in CI (promptfoo, DeepEval or Pydantic Evals). 29. What should an agent trace capture, and why OpenTelemetry?

A trace should let you replay the decision: every model call (input, output, model version, parameters, tokens, latency), every tool call (name, arguments, result, errors), retrieval results, sub-agent spans in a tree, and the final state or outcome, with IDs linking them. That is what makes error analysis, trajectory grading and cost attribution possible. OpenTelemetry matters because it is a vendor-neutral format: Phoenix, Langfuse, MLflow, TruLens and others ingest it, so you can switch or combine tools without re-instrumenting. Anthropic's tool-writing post adds the practical point: track runtime, number of tool calls, tokens and tool errors, not just accuracy, and read what the agent omitted. See Module 15.

30. How do you put agent evals in CI without flakiness or runaway cost?

Tier the suite. On every pull request run a small, fast, deterministic smoke set (regression tasks near 100%, cached or mocked tools, code graders). Nightly, run the full capability suite with several trials per task and judge-based graders. Before a release, run the expensive long-horizon and safety tasks. Gate on confidence intervals rather than raw thresholds (fail only if the regression is outside noise), quarantine flaky tasks with a separate label, and cap tokens and wall-clock per run. promptfoo's GitHub Action and DeepEval's pytest integration post results on the PR; most platforms also score sampled production traffic online. How the labs do it

31. How does Anthropic's RSP differ from OpenAI's Preparedness Framework?

They share the core logic: capability evals act as rule-out tests, and when a lab cannot rule out a dangerous threshold, stronger safeguards are required before deployment. The structure differs. Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy ties safeguard standards (deployment and security) to AI Safety Levels; ASL-3 was first activated in May 2025 for Claude Opus 4 as a precaution, because Anthropic could not rule out CBRN risk. RSP v3.0 (February 2026) specifies only the next level of safeguards rather than a ladder of future levels, adds a non-binding Frontier Safety Roadmap and Risk Reports every 3 to 6 months; the current version (v3.4, July 2026) names CBRN, AI R&D and cyber. OpenAI's Preparedness Framework v2 (April 2025) defines Tracked Categories (biological and chemical, cybersecurity, AI self-improvement) plus Research Categories (such as long-range autonomy and sandbagging), each with High and Critical thresholds; Capabilities Reports and Safeguards Reports go to a Safety Advisory Group, which recommends to leadership. Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework uses Critical Capability Levels with early-warning alert thresholds. Frameworks change often, so cite versions and dates. See Module 16 and Module 17.

32. What is in a system card, and how do you read one critically?

A frontier system card typically covers safeguards and harmlessness, honesty, agentic safety (prompt injection, malicious use), cyber, reward hacking, an alignment assessment, model welfare in Anthropic's case, and the frontier-safety evaluations that decide the safeguard level. Read it critically by asking: which harness and budget, how many trials, are there confidence intervals, which evals were run by third parties, what was the elicitation effort (a weakly elicited model under-reports capability), and what the card says about evaluation awareness (Anthropic's Claude Sonnet 4.5 card reported the model sometimes verbalised that it was being tested, and that probes found internal representations of this). Compare against the previous card for changed definitions. See Module 16.

33. What does METR's time horizon measure, and what does it not?

It measures the length of task (in expert-human time) at which a model's predicted success on METR's software and ML task suite is 50%, from a logistic fit of success against log task length; an 80% horizon is also reported. Time Horizon 1.1 (January 2026) grew the suite from 170 to 228 tasks, with 31 tasks of 8 hours or more, and moved the infrastructure to Inspect; example estimates were about 320 minutes for Claude Opus 4.5 and 214 minutes for GPT-5. It does not mean the model can work autonomously for that long on arbitrary jobs: it is a statistic of one task distribution, confidence intervals are wide at the frontier because there are few very long tasks, and messy, low-context tasks lower performance. See Module 11.

34. What is Epoch AI's Capabilities Index, and why use Item Response Theory?

Individual benchmarks saturate and each covers a narrow slice, so comparing models across years is hard. The Epoch Capabilities Index (ECI) uses Item Response Theory to put 50+ benchmarks on one scale: it jointly estimates each model's ability and each benchmark's difficulty and discrimination, so a new hard benchmark can be linked to old easy ones. The scale is anchored so Claude 3.5 Sonnet is 130 and GPT-5 is 150. It combines Epoch's own runs, developer-reported scores and independent leaderboards, so its quality depends on those inputs. IRT is the same idea standardized tests use to compare students who took different papers. See Module 16.

35. Who evaluates the labs from outside, and what do they add?

METR runs autonomy and time-horizon evaluations and maintains an index of frontier safety policies. The UK AI Security Institute does pre-deployment testing (for example, the joint US/UK test of upgraded Claude 3.5 Sonnet in November 2024) and co-develops Inspect with Meridian Labs. US CAISI (renamed from the US AISI in June 2025) runs voluntary unclassified evaluations of demonstrable risks and compares US and foreign models; its DeepSeek evaluation found the model 12x more susceptible to agent hijacking. Apollo Research studies scheming and deception. Epoch AI and Artificial Analysis run independent benchmarks with published methodology, and Scale's SEAL leaderboards use private datasets to resist overfitting. They add independence, different elicitation, and private test sets. See Module 16.

Safety evals

36. What are dangerous-capability evals, and why are they "rule-out" tests?

They measure whether a model has capabilities that would enable severe harm: chemical and biological uplift, offensive cyber, autonomous AI R&D, and (at Google DeepMind) harmful manipulation. Unlike product evals, the goal is not a high score but a confident negative: showing the model is below a threshold. That flips the statistics. You must elicit the model as strongly as a motivated attacker would (tools, scaffolds, fine-tuning where relevant), because under-elicitation produces false reassurance. When results are ambiguous, the policies say to act as if the threshold might be crossed, which is how ASL-3 came to be activated as a precaution. Google's Gemini 3 Pro report is a good example of honest reporting: no critical level reached, but the cyber alert threshold was met. See Module 17.

37. What is evaluation awareness, and why does it threaten alignment evals?

A model is evaluation-aware when it recognizes that it is being tested. If behavior differs between tests and deployment, alignment evals measure test behavior, not real behavior. Anthropic's Claude Sonnet 4.5 system card reported the model sometimes said it thought it was being tested, and white-box probes found representations of evaluation awareness that grew in strength over training. In the OpenAI and Apollo anti-scheming work (September 2025), covert-action rates fell sharply (o3 from 13% to 0.4%), but evaluation awareness causally contributed to the reduction, so part of the improvement may be "behaves well when it thinks it is watched". Mitigations include more realistic environments, production-like transcripts, interpretability checks, and scanning transcripts for awareness (Inspect Scout does this). See Module 17.

38. How do you evaluate an agent's robustness to prompt injection?

Plant adversarial instructions where the agent will read them (web pages, emails, documents, tool outputs) inside realistic tasks, and measure two numbers together: attack success rate (did it follow the injected instruction, for example exfiltrate data or send money) and utility under attack (does it still finish the user's real task). A defense that blocks attacks by refusing everything is not a defense. Vary attack styles and positions, grade success by environment state (was the email actually sent?), and include benign look-alike instructions to measure over-refusal. Keep the attack set partly private, since public attack sets end up in training data. Lab system cards now report prompt-injection results in their agentic-safety sections, and CAISI's DeepSeek finding (12x more susceptible to agent hijacking) shows how large the gaps can be. See Module 08 and Module 17.

39. What are Petri and Bloom, and when would you use each?

Both came from Anthropic and are now maintained by Meridian Labs. Petri (October 2025) is an automated auditor: an agent runs multi-turn conversations with the target in simulated environments, driven by researcher seed instructions, and an LLM judge scores transcripts on dimensions such as deception, sycophancy, self-preservation and power-seeking; the launch ran 14 models against 111 seeds. Bloom (December 2025) generates a targeted eval for one behavior in four stages (understanding, ideation, rollout, judgment) and outputs a 1 to 10 presence score with a meta-judge. Anthropic's own summary: Bloom suits precise measurement with statistics, Petri suits open-ended exploration. See Module 17.

Pitfalls

40. What is contamination, how do you detect it, and how do you prevent it?

Contamination is test data (questions, answers or solutions) appearing in training data, so the score measures memory, not capability. Detect it by testing verbatim recall (OpenAI found frontier models could reproduce SWE-bench Verified gold patches), comparing performance before and after a training cutoff (LiveCodeBench's time windows), and comparing public vs private splits of the same benchmark. Prevent it with held-out or private splits (SWE-bench Pro's commercial set, ARC's semi-private set, Scale SEAL), fresh continuously collected tasks (LiveCodeBench, SWE-rebench), canary strings, and encrypted answers (BrowseComp). For your own evals: never paste test tasks into prompts, docs or fine-tuning data. See Module 18.

41. What is "The Leaderboard Illusion" about?

A 2025 paper by Singh et al. on Chatbot Arena. It documented undisclosed private testing, in which labs tested many variants and published only the best (Meta tested 27 private variants before Llama 4); unequal data access, where Google and OpenAI each received about 19 to 20% of battles while 83 open-weight models together got about 30%; and silent deprecations that break the Bradley–Terry assumptions. Training on arena-style data could produce relative gains of up to 112% on the arena distribution. The lesson generalizes: best-of-N selection on a noisy leaderboard inflates the published number, just like p-hacking. See Module 18.

42. What is reward hacking in agent evals, and how do you catch it?

The agent satisfies the grader without doing the task: editing or deleting failing tests, special-casing the test inputs, writing to the file the checker reads, or claiming success in the final message. It is an eval bug as much as a model behavior, and Anthropic's system cards report it as its own section. Catch it by grading with checks the agent cannot touch (tests copied in after the run, read-only checker files), diffing protected files, scanning transcripts for suspicious actions, and spot-reading high-scoring transcripts, not just failures. Goodhart's law is the general version: once a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. See Module 18.

43. A benchmark has saturated. What does that mean, and what do you do?

Saturated means top systems are clustered near the ceiling (or near the label-noise floor), so differences are within error bars and the benchmark no longer ranks them. Often the ceiling is below 100% because some labels are wrong: MMLU-Redux estimated about 6.5% of MMLU questions have errors, and OpenAI's audit found most remaining SWE-bench Verified failures were due to flawed tests. Stop using it for frontier comparison, keep it as a regression check, and move to a harder or cleaner successor (MMLU-Pro, HLE, SWE-bench Pro, ScreenSpot-Pro, ARC-AGI-3). If you build benchmarks, design for slow saturation: hard tasks, private splits, and a refresh process. See Module 18.

44. What is construct validity, and give an example of a benchmark that lacks it.

Construct validity asks whether the benchmark measures the capability it claims to. A vision benchmark whose questions can be answered from the text alone measures reading, not seeing; that is why MMMU-Pro removed text-solvable items. An offline web benchmark like Mind2Web replays recorded actions and cannot credit a different valid path, so it partly measures "did you do it the annotator's way". A customer-service benchmark with a noisy user simulator partly measures robustness to the simulator. Test construct validity with ablations (remove the image, change the path, swap the simulator) and by checking correlation with the real-world outcome you care about. See Module 18.

System design

45. Design the eval program for a new agent product, from day zero to launch. design

Weeks 1 to 2: log traces from dogfooding, run error analysis, build a 20 to 50 task regression set with code graders, and put it in CI. Weeks 3 to 6: grow a capability suite from real and synthetic tasks (dimensions → tuples → natural-language tasks), add one narrow, validated judge per persistent failure mode, run several trials per task, and report pass^k and cost. Before launch: red-team for injection and misuse, run safety checks on irreversible actions, and agree ship criteria in writing (for example, no regression outside the CI, and pass^3 of at least X on critical flows). After launch: sample production traces for online scoring and human review, feed new failures back into the suite, and retire saturated tasks. Give the suite a named owner, as Anthropic recommends. See Module 15 and Module 19.

46. Design an eval for a deep-research agent that writes cited reports. design

Split it into verifiable and judged parts. Verifiable: BrowseComp-style questions with short checkable answers (hard to find, easy to verify), and citation checks: does each cited source exist, and does it support the sentence it is attached to? Judged: a rubric for completeness, source quality, synthesis and calibration of uncertainty, validated against expert labels. Use a fixed corpus (as BrowseComp-Plus does) for reproducible comparisons, and live web runs for realism, reported separately because live answers drift. Track tokens, tool calls and time, since Anthropic found token usage explained most of the variance on BrowseComp for its research system. Include questions with no good answer, to catch confident fabrication. See Module 06 and Module 11.

47. Offline evals say 90%, production success is 60%. Diagnose. design

The gap is a distribution shift until proven otherwise. Check, in order: (1) data: sample 100 failed production traces and run error analysis; are these intents, lengths, languages or tools absent from the eval set? (2) environment: mocks that are kinder than real APIs (no timeouts, no rate limits, clean data); (3) users: simulated users that are more cooperative and precise than real ones; (4) grader: an offline judge that is lenient compared with what counts as success in production (re-validate it on production samples); (5) measurement: is "success" defined the same way in both places? Then fold the failures into the offline suite, so next time the offline number moves before production does. See Module 12 and Module 15.

48. How do you choose between two models for production?

Run both through your own suite (public leaderboards measure someone else's distribution), same harness, several trials, paired comparison. Plot quality against cost and against latency and look at the Pareto frontier: a model that is 1 point better but 3x the cost is rarely the right choice unless that point sits on your most valuable flow. Look at the breakdown, not just the mean: the better-on-average model may be worse on a critical slice. Check reliability (pass^k), refusal and safety behavior, and variance. Then confirm with a limited online A/B test, because offline wins do not always transfer. See Module 04 and Module 15.

Building blocks (Part V)

49. Your agent's score went up 5 points after a new release. How do you tell whether the model or the harness gets the credit?

First, remember there are two harnesses: the agent harness (loop, tools, context management, retries, verification) is part of the system under test, and the evaluation harness runs the trials and grades them. Pin the evaluation harness, then run a paired ablation: the same tasks, several trials each, changing one component at a time (new model with old harness, old model with new harness) and report each paired difference with a CI. Then read the transcripts of the tasks that flipped and label each failure; a 2026 taxonomy, "Model or Harness?", argues that many faults sit on the interaction between two components, so a failure can need two labels. Two traps: the winner's curse (if you tried 20 harness variants that do nothing, the best still looks about 3.7 points better when the SE is 2, so confirm on fresh tasks) and gate size (a −2 point gate needs roughly 350 tasks to be meaningful). HAL exists because this question is so common: it runs scaffold × model × benchmark under one setup and reports cost next to accuracy. See Module 21.

50. Two models are 2 points apart on an agentic coding benchmark. Could the machines explain the gap?

Yes, easily. Anthropic's February 2026 engineering post on infrastructure noise reports that sandbox configuration (CPU, memory, time limits, how many trials share a machine) can swing agentic coding scores by several percentage points, sometimes more than the gap between top models. So pin the sandbox resources and report them, interleave both systems on identical infrastructure rather than running one today and one next week, log infrastructure errors (timeouts, out-of-memory kills, failed container starts) separately from agent failures, and put a CI on the difference. If the setups differ, you are comparing two experiments, not two models. See Module 18 and Module 21.

51. How do you evaluate whether a skill triggers when it should, and only then?

Write a trigger eval: about 20 realistic queries, roughly half that should trigger the skill and half that should not, where the negatives are near misses (same vocabulary, different need) rather than easy unrelated prompts. Run each query 3 times and count it as triggered if it fires in at least half the runs; the harness counts a trigger when the first tool call is a Skill call or a read of the skill file. If the description needs work, let an optimizer propose rewrites on a 60/40 stratified split and pick the version by its score on the held-out 40%, not the training part. Then run the uplift eval separately: with-skill and baseline runs launched together, graded by assertions with evidence. A skill's value is roughly P(trigger | relevant) × uplift, minus the cost of false triggers. See Module 22.

52. Design an eval for a personal assistant's long-term memory. design

Memory failures are delayed, silent and compounding, so single-turn tests miss them. Build timelines: scripted multi-session histories that plant facts, then change them ("I moved to Denver"), then ask the user to forget something, then probe much later. Cover all six operations (write, retrieve, update, read, forget, consolidate) and report separate numbers: recall@k for retrieval, staleness rate (answers with the outdated value), false-memory rate (confident claims the user never made), write precision and recall, deletion compliance (including summaries that absorbed the deleted fact), and cross-user leakage. Always run three baselines: no memory, full context (often strong on LoCoMo-sized histories) and oracle retrieval, which tells you whether the loss is in retrieval or in reasoning. Use LongMemEval or MemoryAgentBench for comparison, but do not compare vendor numbers across reports, because setups differ. See Module 23.

53. You are shipping an MCP server to other teams' agents. What do you test? design

Treat the tool definitions as prompts, because they are. Test five rungs separately: selection (right tool, including when to call none), arguments (valid and correct), sequencing, parallel calls and result use. Write contract tests on the server itself (schemas, errors, pagination), then agent-level tasks with prompts that do not name the tool, plus distractor scaling: add similar tools and watch selection accuracy fall. Grade by final state where you can (as Toolathlon and MCPMark do with scripts) and by claims-based rubrics where you cannot (as MCP-Atlas does). On the security side, test tool poisoning, over-broad permissions and the rug pull (the description changes after approval): the defense is to pin a hash of the tool manifest and re-list tools after a list_changed notice, and your eval should assert that the client blocks or re-prompts. See Module 24 and Module 25.

Beyond the core (Part VI)

54. A vendor says its agent has a 2% prompt injection success rate. What do you ask?

Five questions, each a way a single ASR number misleads. Which attacker? A fixed, static attack set, or an adaptive one; in NIST and CAISI's January 2025 work, attack success on new injection tasks rose from about 11% with the strongest built-in attack to about 81% with new red-team attacks. How many tries? Attack pass@k compounds: 5% per try is about 40% at 10 tries and about 72% at 25; the NIST work saw about 57% rise to about 80% with 25 attempts. Per task, or averaged? A harmless average can hide one catastrophic task. What did the defense cost? Report utility and utility under attack alongside targeted ASR, as AgentDojo does. Is it secure or just weak? "Security by incompetence" is low ASR because the agent cannot finish the attacker's goal either. Then check the design: an agent with private data, untrusted content and a way to send data out has the lethal trifecta, and needs an architectural answer, not a filter. See Module 25.

55. How do you grade a 20-page deep-research report, and keep the cost sane?

Separate report quality from citation quality. DeepResearch Bench does this with RACE, which scores the report against a reference report on four dimensions with task-adaptive weights and reports target ÷ (target + reference), so 0.5 means as good as the reference, and FACT, which checks each (statement, URL) pair and reports citation accuracy and effective citations. DEER adds a document-level fact check that covers uncited claims too. Keep faithfulness (does the source say this?) apart from factuality (is it true?): a faithful citation of a wrong source passes one and fails the other. On cost, a naive design can use about 990,000 judge tokens per report, so use passage retrieval instead of whole pages, a cascade (cheap check first, strong judge on the uncertain cases) and stratified sampling of claims, and validate each cheaper stage against the full one. See Module 26.

56. Design an eval for a health assistant that answers the public. design

Start with the risk tier: advice straight to non-experts is a high tier, so you need expert rubrics, abstention and escalation tests, a critical-failure rate and measured agreement. Follow the HealthBench pattern: physicians write conversation-specific criteria with points from about −10 to +10; the score is met points ÷ total positive points, clipped to 0 to 1. Double-grade 100 to 300 items and compute Cohen's kappa, because raw agreement flatters when most answers are fine; the model grader should agree with physicians about as well as physicians agree with each other. Weight by severity, and treat critical failures (a missed emergency, a dangerous dose) as a gate, not a term in an average. Report coverage and abstention together, so refusing everything does not look safe. Refresh the suite as guidelines change. See Module 27.

57. What makes eval evidence strong enough for a regulator or a safety case?

Five things change when the audience changes: purpose (support a claim with a direction and threshold, not "which is better?"), evidence standard (the conservative side of the CI, plus proof you tried hard to make it fail), documentation, reproducibility (pinned snapshot, harness, prompts, seeds, data hashes) and independence. A safety case organizes this as claims, arguments and evidence, lists defeaters openly, and uses one of four argument families (inability, control, trustworthiness, deference; inability is the most established). The margin test is the classic trap: 2 solved out of 40 is a 5% point estimate but a one-sided 95% upper bound of about 15%, so a "below 10%" claim does not hold yet. For EU systemic-risk models, Article 55 asks for model evaluation including adversarial testing, and the GPAI Code of Practice adds detail; ISO/IEC 42001 certifies the management system, not the model. See Module 28.

Reliability, cost and the measurement itself

58. Two agents both score 70%. Why might one be far more reliable?

Because accuracy is one number and reliability is several. "Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability" (the Princeton HAL group, ICML 2026) proposes four dimensions: consistency (does it behave the same way twice; pass^k is one measure), robustness (does it survive paraphrases, noise and faults), predictability (can you anticipate its failures, for example through calibrated confidence) and safety (how bad are the failures). Its headline finding, as the authors summarize it, is that capability has improved faster than reliability. So report the four side by side, each with its own bootstrap error bar over tasks. See Module 04.

59. Full benchmark runs cost too much. How do you cut cost without fooling yourself?

Two options. Stratified subsampling: run a random sample from each stratum and use the finite population correction, SE = √(p(1−p)/n × (N−n)/(N−1)); with N = 500 and n = 100 the 95% half-width is about ±8.6 points, which is fine for spotting large regressions and useless for 2-point decisions. Anchor sets with IRT: fit task difficulty and discrimination once from many past full runs (the 2PL model, P = 1/(1 + e^(−a(θ−b)))), then run a new system on a small anchor set and estimate its full score; tinyBenchmarks reports about 100 examples getting within about 2% on popular LLM benchmarks, and "Efficient Benchmarking of AI Agents" (2026) applies mid-range task selection to agents. Good anchors are tasks near a 50% solve rate for the systems you care about. Keep full runs for checking the estimator and for decisions that matter. See Module 04.

60. How would you measure whether a model knows it is being evaluated?

As a classifier test. Mix transcripts from evals and from real use, hide the labels, and ask the model (or probe its activations) which is which; AUC summarizes how well it separates them, and a human baseline tells you how obvious the evals are to people too. Needham et al. (2025) built such a benchmark from transcripts drawn from dozens of public datasets, and a 2025 probing paper reports that eval-like and deployment-like prompts are linearly separable inside an open-weights model. Remember that detection is not the same as behavior change: the next step is a cue on/off comparison, the same scenario with and without the test-like cues, to see whether behavior shifts. EvalDetectBench (September 2026) is a dedicated benchmark but very recent, so read it before quoting it. Use the result practically: find your most test-like scenarios and make them realistic. See Module 17.

61. What does an AI control evaluation measure, and how is it different from a capability eval?

A capability eval asks what the model can do. A control evaluation (Greenblatt et al., 2023) asks whether your deployment protocol stays safe even if the model tries its hardest to misbehave. A red team plays the untrusted model U and tries to slip something past, such as a backdoor in APPS coding solutions; a blue team designs the protocol with a weaker trusted model T (trusted monitoring, defer to trusted, trusted editing, untrusted monitoring) and a limited human audit budget. The result is two numbers, safety and usefulness, traded off along a curve. SHADE-Arena applies a related idea to agents: a main task plus a hidden side task, with a monitor scoring suspicion; at release the best monitor reached an AUC of about 0.87. Chain-of-thought monitorability is the related bet that reasoning stays readable, which Korbak et al. call a "fragile opportunity". UK AISI's ControlArena packages these setups. See Module 17.

62. Your team runs DSPy or GEPA against the eval suite and the score keeps climbing. What worries you?

Goodhart's law on fast-forward. An optimizer tries hundreds or thousands of variants, so it finds the grader's quirks far faster than a person would, and RL training on the same grader turns those quirks into learned behavior. If the grader is an unvalidated LLM judge, the optimizer will find its biases (length, confident tone, keywords). The winner's curse also applies: the best of many variants looks better than it is. Defenses: validate the grader before optimizing against it, keep a held-out set the optimizer never sees and treat that score as the real one, read transcripts of the winning variant for shortcuts, and refresh the held-out set if it gets used for decisions too often. The same logic applies to a skill description optimizer, which is why it selects by the held-out score. See Module 18 and Module 22.

63. Before you trust a new agentic benchmark, what do you check?

Run the Agentic Benchmark Checklist (Zhu et al., NeurIPS 2025): task validity (can the task be solved only by the intended skill?), outcome validity (does the grader's pass mean real success?) and reporting (are limitations and uncertainty stated?). The authors found real problems in popular benchmarks, including insufficient tests in SWE-bench Verified and τ-bench counting empty responses as successes, with misestimation of up to 100% in relative terms. Three cheap smoke tests catch many of these: a null agent that does nothing, an echo agent that restates the task and takes no action, and the reference solution run through the grader. The first two should score about zero; the reference solution should score about 100%. See Module 18.

What separates a senior answer

Across all 63 answers, three habits recur: (1) every number comes with its uncertainty and its conditions (harness, trials, date); (2) the grader is treated as a measuring instrument that must itself be validated; (3) the answer ends with a decision or a next check, not with a score. If you do these three things, your answers will sound expert even on topics you know less well.

How much a model increases a person's ability to do something (for example, a harmful task) compared with a control group without it. Module 17

Analogy: learning to cook

Reading every cookbook does not make you a chef. Cooking the same dish three times, tasting carefully and changing one thing each time does. The 30-day plan is three dishes, a calibrated palate (the judge), and a blind tasting (the paired test).

Key takeaways

  1. One picture holds the field. Datasets, the system and an environment feed an eval run; graders turn transcripts into scores; metrics and statistics turn scores into evidence; evidence drives a decision. Pitfalls are whatever makes that evidence lie.
  2. Answer any design question with the six-step frame: decision, system, data, grader, number with uncertainty, threats. Then say how you would iterate.
  3. Climb the grader ladder in order: code first, a narrow judge validated with TPR and TNR where code cannot see, humans for subjective or high-stakes calls and for calibration labels.
  4. Pick the metric from the decision. pass@k when something picks the best attempt, pass^k when every attempt must work, win rates for preferences, precision and recall for detectors, and always cost and latency beside quality.
  5. Know the size of the noise before you argue. A single score on 500 tasks is about ±4.4 points; quadruple the tasks to halve that, and compare systems with a paired test.
  6. When a number moves, debug in order: noise, measurement changes, infrastructure, slices, then transcripts. Most surprises die in the first two checks.
  7. Every claim needs its conditions: harness, budget, trials, error bars and date. Frameworks, leaderboards and tools change monthly, so read the primary source and cite the version.
  8. Parts V and VI reuse the same logic under more pressure. Evaluate one building block at a time with paired ablations; report security as utility and attack success at a stated budget; grade long reports on quality and citations separately; and when a regulator is the audience, use the conservative side of the interval and write the evidence down as a safety case.

Check yourself

1. An autonomous support agent succeeds on a task type 90% of the time. Which number best describes how often it gets all five of a customer's similar requests right?

pass^5 = 0.9^5 ≈ 0.59. pass@k answers a different question (at least one success), which is the right one only when something downstream can pick the good attempt.

2. A vendor reports a 4-point SWE-bench Verified gain. What is the most important first question?

Harness and budget often move scores more than the model; 500 tasks give about ±4 points at 95% near 50%; and OpenAI's 2026 audit found contamination and flawed tests, so fresh or private tasks (SWE-bench Pro, SWE-rebench) are more trustworthy.

3. Your judge has TPR 0.9 and TNR 0.8 and reports an 80% pass rate. The corrected estimate is closest to:

θ = (0.80 + 0.80 − 1) / (0.90 + 0.80 − 1) = 0.60 / 0.70 ≈ 0.857. False passes (TNR 0.8) push the raw rate up, but at this pass rate the false fails (TPR 0.9) push it down more, so the corrected value is higher than what the judge reported.

4. Which statement about METR's 50% time horizon is correct?

It comes from a logistic fit of success against log human task time on a software and ML task distribution. METR explicitly warns against reading it as "the model can work for N hours".

5. In a safety (dangerous-capability) eval, why is weak elicitation a problem?

These are rule-out tests: the goal is a confident negative. If the model was not pushed as hard as a motivated user could push it, a low score does not rule anything out.

6. On DeepResearch Bench, an agent's report gets a RACE score of 0.45. What does that mean?

RACE is reference-relative: target ÷ (target + reference), so 0.5 means as good as the reference and 0.45 means a little worse. Citations are scored separately by FACT.

7. An injection succeeds 5% of the time per attempt, and the attacker can try 20 times. About how likely is at least one success?

Attack pass@k = 1 − 0.95^20 ≈ 0.64. A per-attempt ASR understates risk whenever attackers can retry, which is why the attacker budget belongs next to the number.

8. A description optimizer produced five candidate skill descriptions on a 60/40 split. Which one should you ship?

Selecting on the data you tuned on rewards overfitting (and the winner's curse). The held-out score is the honest estimate of how the description will trigger on new queries.

9. A safety case claims the model solves at most 10% of cyber tasks. It solved 2 of 40. Does the claim hold?

Regulator-facing evidence uses the conservative side of the interval. With 40 tasks, 10% cannot be ruled out, so you need more tasks or a stronger argument.
