Deep research and real-work agents

Some agents do not end with a short answer or a passing test. They end with a 20-page cited report, a slide deck, a spreadsheet, a video edit or a finished ticket in a company's systems. This module is about grading that kind of output: how to score a long report for coverage and insight, how to check every citation automatically, how the real-work benchmarks (GDPval, the Remote Labor Index, TheAgentCompany, Toolathlon) grade deliverables, and how to build all of it yourself without spending more on the judge than on the agent.

Advanced~70 minPrereqs: Modules 06, 11, 13

By the end of this module you can

  • Explain what a deep-research agent produces and name the five reasons a long cited report is hard to grade.
  • Separate the quality dimensions of a report: coverage, depth, faithfulness, citation accuracy, citation coverage, source quality, recency, instruction following and readability.
  • Describe how DeepResearch Bench scores reports (RACE: reference-based, adaptive criteria; FACT: citation accuracy and effective citations), and place DEER, ResearchRubrics, Mind2Web 2, BrowseComp-Plus and others on a map.
  • Build a claim-extraction and citation-verification pipeline, and control its cost with passage retrieval, cascades and sampling.
  • Explain how real-work benchmarks grade unstructured deliverables (expert pairwise comparison, rubrics, automated state checks) and what an automation rate does and does not mean.
  • Run a blinded pairwise expert-comparison study and put a confidence interval on the result.

Two families of agents, one grading problem

Earlier modules mostly graded things a computer can check. A tool call either matches the expected arguments or it does not (Module 06). A patch either passes the hidden tests or it does not (Module 07). Even BrowseComp, which asks an agent to search the web for a long time, keeps grading cheap by choosing questions whose answer is one short string: "hard to find, easy to verify" (Module 06, Module 08).

This module covers two families of agents that break that comfortable pattern:

  • Deep-research agents take an open question ("How will falling battery prices change grid storage in Europe over the next five years?"), plan a search strategy, read dozens or hundreds of web pages, and write a long, structured report with citations. OpenAI's Deep Research, Google's Gemini Deep Research, Perplexity's research mode and Claude's Research feature are all products of this kind. The architecture behind Claude's version is the case study in Module 11.
  • Real-work agents do the kind of task a person is paid for: draft a legal memo, build a financial model, design a floor plan, edit a product video, or close a ticket that requires reading a wiki, asking a colleague and updating three internal systems. Their output is a deliverable: a file, a set of files, or a changed state in some software.

What unites them is that the output has no single right answer, is long, and is expensive to check. The picture below orders the kinds of output from easiest to hardest to grade, with one twist on the far right.

Analogy: the exam, the term paper and the internship

A short-answer benchmark is a multiple-choice exam: a machine grades it. A deep-research report is a term paper: a teacher needs a rubric, and must check the bibliography. A real-work deliverable is an internship project: the best judge is an experienced professional who asks "would I accept this from a colleague?" This module is about grading term papers and internships at scale.

What a deep-research agent produces

A deep-research run usually has four phases: plan (break the question into sub-questions), search and read (many queries, many pages, often in parallel subagents), synthesize (organize findings into sections with an argument), and cite (attach each claim to the source that supports it). The output is typically thousands of words with dozens of inline citations.

Each phase leaves its own fingerprints on the report, and each fingerprint needs a different check. The figure annotates an excerpt of a report with the problems a good eval must catch.

Why grading a long report is hard

Five problems stack on top of each other:

  1. No single right answer. Two excellent reports on the same question can be organized differently, emphasize different evidence and reach different (defensible) conclusions. Exact-match grading is useless, and even "compare with a reference answer" has to allow for legitimate variation.
  2. Length. A 20-page report is roughly 10,000 words. A judge model can read it, but judges get less reliable as inputs get longer, and they drift toward rewarding length itself (verbosity bias, Module 13).
  3. Citations are claims about the world. Checking that "[4]" supports sentence 5 means opening source 4, finding the relevant passage and reading it. Multiply by 60 or 100 citations per report.
  4. The world moves. Pages change or disappear (link rot), facts go out of date, and a reference answer written in March may be wrong by September. Live-web evals are not perfectly reproducible.
  5. Judging costs real money. If verifying one report takes a million judge tokens, a suite of 100 tasks, 3 runs each, across 2 agent versions is 600 million tokens. Cost control is a design requirement, not an afterthought (see controlling judge cost below).
The trap: "looks thorough" is not "is correct"

Long reports with many citations and confident headings impress both humans and model judges. Anthropic's team noted in its research-system post that human testers caught agents preferring SEO-optimized content farms over authoritative sources, something their automated evals had missed (Module 11). A grading scheme that rewards the look of rigor will be optimized for the look of rigor.

Before choosing a benchmark or writing a rubric, name what "good" means. Most published schemes use some subset of these dimensions. The first column is the vocabulary you should be fluent in.

Two of these are routinely confused: faithfulness and factuality. Faithfulness asks "does the cited source say this?"; factuality asks "is this true?". They are different axes, and a claim can fail one while passing the other.

DeepResearch Bench: RACE and FACT

DeepResearch Bench (Du et al., June 2025, arXiv 2506.11763) is the most widely cited benchmark for research reports, and its two scoring frameworks are worth understanding in detail because you can reuse both ideas. It contains 100 PhD-level research tasks across 22 fields, written by domain experts, with the topic mix chosen to reflect what real users ask deep-research tools. It splits evaluation into two independent frameworks: one for the report's quality, one for its citations.

RACE: grading report quality against a reference

RACE stands for Reference-based Adaptive Criteria-driven Evaluation. Three ideas make it work:

  1. Fixed dimensions. Every report is judged on four: comprehensiveness, insight/depth, instruction following and readability.
  2. Adaptive weights and criteria. A judge model reads the task and decides how much each dimension matters for this task, then writes task-specific criteria (with their own weights) under each dimension. A literature survey might weight comprehensiveness heavily; a "should we enter this market?" memo might weight insight.
  3. Reference-relative scoring. Instead of asking for an absolute score (which judges give inconsistently), the judge scores the target report and a high-quality reference report on the same criteria, and the final number is the target's share of the combined score. A result of 0.5 means "as good as the reference".

Why the ratio instead of the raw 6.75? Because absolute scores from a judge drift: the same report might get 6.5 today and 7.2 after a prompt tweak, and different tasks have different natural score ranges. Anchoring every score to a reference that the judge sees in the same call cancels much of that drift. The price is that you need a good reference report per task, and the score becomes "relative to that reference", so a mediocre reference inflates everyone.

FACT: grading the citations

FACT (Framework for Factual Abundance and Citation Trustworthiness) ignores the prose quality and asks two questions about the citations: are they right, and how many useful ones are there?

The paper describes using a web-scraping service to fetch cited pages and an LLM judge to decide support. At release it reported that agents differed a lot on both metrics and that the two did not move together: some systems cited heavily with lower accuracy, others cited less but more precisely. The lesson for your own evals: a single citation number is gameable. Pair a precision-like metric with a volume or coverage metric.

Name collision alert

"Deep Research Bench" (with a space, by FutureSearch, arXiv 2506.06287) is a different benchmark from "DeepResearch Bench" (Du et al.). FutureSearch's version focuses on web-research tasks such as finding numbers, validating claims and compiling datasets, and runs agents against RetroSearch, a frozen snapshot of scraped web pages, so results are reproducible. Always check which one a leaderboard means.

DEER and the wider benchmark map

DEER (arXiv 2512.17776, first posted December 2025, an ICML 2026 poster) targets expert-level report generation. It pushes on two weaknesses of earlier report benchmarks. First, judges who are not domain experts miss subtle problems, so DEER builds an expert-grounded rubric taxonomy of dimensions and sub-dimensions, and supplies task-specific expert guidance to the judge so it knows what a strong answer must contain. Second, citation checkers like FACT only look at claims that carry a citation, so DEER adds document-level fact-checking that extracts and verifies claims across the whole report, cited and uncited, and assesses the quality of the evidence behind them. If your agent writes "Most analysts expect growth" with no citation, FACT never sees it; DEER-style checking does.

Several other benchmarks cover different corners of the space. Each one below was confirmed to exist at the linked address; details beyond what is written here should be read from the papers.

Why BrowseComp-Plus matters beyond its size

On the live web, a deep-research score mixes two things: how good the search engine was that day, and how good the agent is at using it. BrowseComp-Plus fixes the document collection (with human-verified supporting pages and deliberately hard distractors), so you can swap the retriever while holding the agent fixed, or the reverse. That is the same ablation logic as Module 21: hold everything constant except the component you are measuring.

Grading methods, in depth

The benchmarks above are built from a small number of reusable grading methods. Knowing the methods lets you design your own eval instead of borrowing a benchmark that does not fit your product.

1. Rubric-based report grading

A rubric is a list of specific, checkable criteria, each with a weight. Good report rubrics share a few traits, many learned from PaperBench's hierarchical rubrics (Module 11) and from the judge-design rules in Module 13:

  • Task-specific, not generic. "Report is comprehensive" is a vibe. "States the 2024 average battery pack price and its year-on-year change" is checkable.
  • Binary per item. Met or not met, with evidence quoted from the report. Binary items agree better across judges and humans than 1-to-10 scales.
  • Mandatory versus optional. Some items are gates: missing them fails the report regardless of the total. ResearchRubrics distinguishes mandatory from optional items for this reason.
  • Negative items. Penalize known bad behaviors ("presents a forecast as established fact", "relies on a single source for the central claim").
  • One criterion per judge call, or at most one dimension per call, so criteria do not bleed into each other.

Worked example, with the rubric from the code later in this module: criteria C1 (weight 3, mandatory), C2 (2), C3 (2), C4 (1, mandatory) and a negative item N1 (-3). Positive weights sum to 8. A report that meets C1, C2 and C4 but also triggers N1 scores (3 + 2 + 1 - 3) / 8 = 0.375 and passes the gate. A report that meets C2, C3 and C4 but misses C1 scores 5 / 8 = 0.625 but fails the gate, because a mandatory item is missing. The higher number is the worse report.

2. Reference-report comparison

Instead of (or as well as) absolute rubric scores, compare the agent's report to a reference report written by an expert or a strong system, as RACE does. Two flavors:

  • Relative scoring: score both on the same criteria in one call and report the ratio. Stabilizes the judge.
  • Pairwise preference: ask "which is better, A or B, or tie?" with positions randomized. This is how GDPval grades deliverables, and it is the basis of the protocol later in this module.

Watch for three problems. The reference can be wrong or dated, and then the eval rewards matching its mistakes. Judges have position bias (Module 13), so always run both orders or randomize. And if the reference was written by the same model family as the agent, the judge may favor the shared style (self-preference).

3. Claim extraction and citation verification

This is the engine under FACT, DEER's fact-checking and most production citation checks. It descends from a line of research you should know by name:

  • AIS, "Attributable to Identified Sources" (Rashkin et al.): a human-annotation framework for asking "does this source support this statement?".
  • ALCE (Gao et al., 2023): automatic citation recall (is each statement fully supported by its citations?) and citation precision (is each citation actually needed and relevant?), scored with an NLI model.
  • FActScore (Min et al., 2023): split a long text into atomic facts and check each against a knowledge source.
  • SAFE and LongFact (Wei et al., 2024): an LLM agent verifies each atomic fact by issuing search queries; the paper combines precision with recall against a target number of facts (F1@K), so a response is not rewarded simply for saying less.

NLI (natural language inference) models are small classifiers that take a premise (the source passage) and a hypothesis (the claim) and output "entails", "contradicts" or "neutral". They are fast and cheap, which makes them useful as a first filter.

4. Faithfulness, coverage and recency as separate numbers

  • Faithfulness is the output of the pipeline above. It needs no ground truth, only the sources.
  • Coverage needs a list of what a good answer must contain. The standard trick, used in TREC's retrieval-augmented generation evaluations, is a list of nuggets: short atomic facts an expert expects ("LFP share rising", "pack price fell about 20%"). A judge marks each nugget present or absent. Coverage = nuggets present ÷ nuggets expected, with vital nuggets weighted more than nice-to-have ones.
  • Recency is best tested with time-sensitive probes: tasks where you know the latest value of something (last quarter's figure, the current version of a standard) and check whether the report uses it. Date-stamp these probes and refresh them, or they become recency traps for the grader too.

5. Controlling judge cost

Here is an illustrative budget for fully checking one 20-page report. Assume 10,000 words (about 13,000 tokens), 30 rubric criteria and 150 cited claims.

  • Naive rubric pass: one call per criterion, each reading the whole report: 30 × 13,000 = 390,000 input tokens.
  • Naive citation pass: each claim sent with its full source page (say 4,000 tokens): 150 × 4,000 = 600,000 tokens.
  • Total: about 990,000 tokens per report. For 100 tasks × 3 runs × 2 agent versions, that is about 594 million tokens, before any human review.

Five techniques bring this down without losing the signal you care about:

  1. Retrieve the passage, not the page. Chunk each source once, cache it by URL, and send only the best-matching passage (about 800 tokens) with each claim.
  2. Cascade. Resolve easy cases with cheap checks first: exact quote and number matching, then a small NLI model, and only send the ambiguous remainder to a large judge, and a thin slice of the hardest to humans.
  3. Section-scoped criteria. Most rubric items concern one section. Send the judge that section (about 3,000 tokens), not the whole report.
  4. Sample claims. To estimate citation accuracy you do not need every claim. Checking a random sample of 50 of 150 claims and observing 80% supported gives a standard error of about √(0.8 × 0.2 / 50) × √(1 − 50/150) ≈ 0.046, so roughly ±9 points at 95% (the second square root is the finite-population correction, because you sampled a third of all claims). Good enough to compare agents; not good enough to certify one report.
  5. Stratify effort by risk. Verify every claim in the executive summary and every number; sample the rest.
A cheaper judge that is wrong is not cheaper

Every cost-cutting step changes the grader, so each one needs its own validation against human labels (TPR and TNR, Module 13). A sampled estimate with a biased judge is a precise wrong number.

Real-work benchmarks

Now the second family. These benchmarks ask whether agents can do work people are paid for. Module 11 covered GDPval and Vending-Bench; here we add their relatives and, more importantly, compare how each one grades.

GDPval, briefly

OpenAI's GDPval (September 2025, arXiv 2510.04374) has 1,320 tasks across 44 occupations drawn from industries that each make up over 5% of US GDP, with a 220-task open gold set. Each task comes from an experienced professional, with a real deliverable (a legal brief, a nursing care plan, a slide deck). Grading is blinded expert pairwise comparison: another professional from the same occupation sees the human deliverable and the model's deliverable, without labels, and picks the better one or calls a tie. The headline metric is the win-or-tie rate against the human expert. OpenAI also released an experimental automated grader and reported its agreement with human graders as somewhat below human-to-human agreement: good for iteration, not a replacement. Artificial Analysis runs GDPval-AA, a leaderboard that runs models on the GDPval tasks through its own agentic harness.

Remote Labor Index (RLI)

The Remote Labor Index (Scale AI and the Center for AI Safety, October 2025, arXiv 2510.26787, Scale blog) asks a blunter question than GDPval: of real freelance projects, what fraction could an AI agent complete to a standard a client would accept? The benchmark collects 240 real projects that freelancers had actually been commissioned and paid to do, across many kinds of remote work (for example design, video and audio, architecture, game development, data analysis and writing). Each project comes with the original brief, the input files, and the human professional's deliverable, which serves as the gold standard.

Agents receive the brief and files and produce their own deliverable. Trained evaluators compare it holistically with the human deliverable and judge whether it would be accepted by a reasonable client as the commissioned work. The headline metric, the automation rate, is the share of projects where the AI's work meets that bar. At launch the best agent tested (Manus) reached an automation rate of 2.5%; later CAIS updates on the live leaderboard report a significant increase, so check the current leaderboard rather than quoting the launch number. RLI also reports an Elo score from pairwise comparisons between AI deliverables, which shows relative progress even while absolute automation stays low.

TheAgentCompany

TheAgentCompany (Xu et al., Carnegie Mellon and collaborators, December 2024, arXiv 2412.14161, NeurIPS 2025 Datasets and Benchmarks) takes the "live system" route. It builds a self-hosted simulated software company with a code host (GitLab), a file store (OwnCloud), a project tracker (Plane), a chat server (RocketChat) and simulated colleagues, LLM-backed characters the agent can message to ask for information. Its 175 tasks span software engineering, project management, finance, HR, admin and data science.

Grading is by checkpoints: each task lists intermediate milestones, verified by scripts that inspect the environment's state (and, for a few subjective checkpoints, by an LLM judge). The paper defines full completion (all checkpoints pass) and a partial-completion score:

S_partial = 0.5 × (checkpoints passed / total checkpoints) + 0.5 × S_full
where S_full = 1 if every checkpoint passes, else 0

The design deliberately rewards finishing: half of the credit is only available for complete success. At first release the best agent fully completed about a quarter of the tasks (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, 24%); the updated paper reports higher numbers for newer models, still far from most tasks. The paper also observed that tasks involving talking to colleagues and navigating complex web interfaces were among the hardest, while many coding tasks were easier.

Toolathlon

Toolathlon, "The Tool Decathlon" (October 2025, arXiv 2510.25726, ICLR 2026), spans 32 software applications and 604 tools (many via MCP servers) with 108 tasks that each need many tool calls across several apps: think "read the course roster in the learning system, compute grades in a spreadsheet, and email students". Two design choices make it relevant here. Environments start from realistic initial states (populated accounts, not empty sandboxes), and every task has a dedicated execution-based evaluation script that checks the final state. Like TheAgentCompany, it turns "real work" into something a script can grade, at the cost of building and maintaining the environments. It also appears in Module 24 from the tools angle.

How unstructured deliverables are graded

Strip away the names and there are only three grading approaches for a deliverable, and every benchmark above is one or a blend of them.

In practice, use them in layers:

  1. Automated gates first. Does the file exist, open, have the requested format and sheets, do the formulas compute, are the numbers right where you know them? A deliverable that fails a gate should not cost an expert's time.
  2. Rubrics for everyday iteration. Written once by experts, applied by a validated judge on every change, with evidence per item. This is where most of your signal comes from week to week.
  3. Expert pairwise studies for milestones. Before a launch or a claim like "matches professionals", run a blinded comparison against human deliverables. It is slow and expensive, and it is the only one of the three that measures what the customer will actually experience.

What "automation rate" and "win rate" mean, and what they do not

Three headline numbers get confused in press coverage. Be precise about which is which:

Win-or-tie rate (GDPval)
Share of comparisons where experts judged the AI deliverable as good as or better than a professional's. 50% would mean parity with the human expert on those tasks.
Automation rate (RLI)
Share of projects where the AI deliverable would be accepted as the commissioned work. An absolute bar, not a comparison: "good enough to hand over?"
Task success (TheAgentCompany, Toolathlon)
Share of tasks whose final state passes the checks. Only as meaningful as the checks and the environment's realism.
What none of them measure
Jobs replaced. They cover sampled, one-shot, well-specified tasks, not the meetings, iterations, context gathering and accountability around them.

Also remember the statistics. RLI's launch figure of 2.5% on 240 projects corresponds to 6 projects. The 95% Wilson interval for 6 of 240 is about 1.2% to 5.3%: the leader could plausibly be at half or double that value. For a 40-task internal study with 12 successes (30%), the interval is about 18% to 45%. When two agents' intervals overlap this much, "agent A automates more" is not a finding (Module 04).

Why pairwise against a human, rather than a score?

Experts disagree on absolute scores ("is this a 6 or a 7?") far more than on comparisons ("is A better than B?"), and a human deliverable is a concrete anchor that keeps the bar realistic. The costs: every task needs a real human deliverable, the comparison says nothing about why one side won unless you collect reasons, and ties must be allowed and reported, or graders are forced into noisy coin flips.

Build your own

Here are the three pieces you need for your own research-agent or real-work eval: a rubric grader, a citation checker, and a pairwise study. The code runs as written (the judge and fetch calls are mocked), so you can test the logic before wiring in real models.

1. A research-agent eval with a rubric

Start with 20 to 50 real questions from your users, stratified by type (fact-finding, comparison, open analysis), exactly as Anthropic's research team did (Module 11). For each, have a domain expert write 5 to 15 rubric items, mark the mandatory ones, and add negative items for the failure modes you saw in error analysis (Module 12).

# Rubric for one task. Weights are relative; negative items are penalties.
RUBRIC = {
  "task_id": "ev-battery-2025",
  "criteria": [
    {"id": "C1", "dim": "coverage",     "weight": 3, "mandatory": True,
     "text": "States the 2024 average battery pack price and its year-on-year change"},
    {"id": "C2", "dim": "coverage",     "weight": 2, "mandatory": False,
     "text": "Compares at least two chemistries (for example LFP and NMC)"},
    {"id": "C3", "dim": "insight",      "weight": 2, "mandatory": False,
     "text": "Explains at least one cause of the price trend, with a source"},
    {"id": "C4", "dim": "instructions", "weight": 1, "mandatory": True,
     "text": "Ends with a one-paragraph recommendation, as the user asked"},
    {"id": "N1", "dim": "faithfulness", "weight": -3, "mandatory": False,
     "text": "Presents a forecast as an established fact"},
  ]
}

JUDGE_TEMPLATE = """You are grading one criterion of a research report.
Criterion: {criterion}
Report section:
<report>
{section}
</report>
Quote the exact sentence(s) that meet the criterion, or say NONE.
Then answer on the last line with MET or NOT_MET."""

def judge_criterion(criterion, section, call_judge):
    """call_judge(prompt) -> str is your model call (see Module 13 for a full,
    validated implementation with retries and structured output)."""
    out = call_judge(JUDGE_TEMPLATE.format(criterion=criterion["text"], section=section))
    return out.strip().splitlines()[-1].strip() == "MET"

def score(verdicts, rubric=RUBRIC):
    """verdicts: {criterion_id: True/False}. Negative items subtract when triggered."""
    pos = sum(c["weight"] for c in rubric["criteria"] if c["weight"] > 0)
    raw = sum(c["weight"] for c in rubric["criteria"] if verdicts.get(c["id"]))
    missed = [c["id"] for c in rubric["criteria"]
              if c["mandatory"] and not verdicts.get(c["id"])]
    return {"score": max(raw, 0) / pos, "passes_gate": not missed,
            "missed_mandatory": missed}

print(score({"C1": True, "C2": True, "C3": False, "C4": True, "N1": True}))
# {'score': 0.375, 'passes_gate': True, 'missed_mandatory': []}
print(score({"C1": False, "C2": True, "C3": True, "C4": True, "N1": False}))
# {'score': 0.625, 'passes_gate': False, 'missed_mandatory': ['C1']}

Report three things per agent version: the gate pass rate (share of reports with no missing mandatory items), the mean score among all reports, and per-dimension means so you can see whether a change helped coverage but hurt faithfulness. Validate the judge on 50 or more expert-labeled (criterion, report) pairs before trusting it.

def extract_claims(report):

    Real pipelines ask an LLM to split sentences into atomic facts (FActScore style)."""
    claims = []
    for sent in re.split(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+", report.strip()):
        cites = [int(n) for n in re.findall(r"\[(\d+)\]", sent)]
        claims.append(Claim(re.sub(r"\s*\[\d+\]", "", sent).strip(), cites))
    return claims

def fetch(url):
    """Step 2: retrieve the cited source (mocked). None = unreachable."""
    return MOCK_WEB.get(url)

STOP = {"the", "a", "of", "in", "to", "by", "and", "about", "was", "were", "made", "up"}
def tokens(s):
    return {w for w in re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", s.lower()) if w not in STOP}

def supports(claim, source):
    """Step 3: cheap first-stage check. Numbers must match; then word overlap.
    Ambiguous cases are escalated, not guessed."""
    c, s = tokens(claim), tokens(source)
    if not all(n in s for n in re.findall(r"\d+", claim)):
        return "unsupported"          # a number in the claim is not in the source
    overlap = len(c & s) / max(len(c), 1)
    if overlap >= 0.8:
        return "supported"
    if overlap >= 0.5:
        return "needs_judge"          # send to NLI model / LLM judge (next stage)
    return "unsupported"

def check(report, refs):
    rows = []
    for cl in extract_claims(report):
        if not cl.cites:
            rows.append((cl.text, None, "uncited"))
            continue
        for n in cl.cites:
            page = fetch(refs.get(n, ""))
            rows.append((cl.text, n, "unreachable" if page is None else supports(cl.text, page)))
    return rows

rows = check(REPORT, REFS)
for text, n, verdict in rows:
    print(f"[{n or '-'}] {verdict:12s} {text[:52]}")

cited = [r for r in rows if r[1] is not None]
checkable = [r for r in cited if r[2] != "unreachable"]
ok = [r for r in checkable if r[2] == "supported"]
print(f"citation accuracy : {len(ok)}/{len(checkable)} = {len(ok)/len(checkable):.2f}")
print(f"effective cites   : {len({r[1] for r in ok})}")
print(f"unreachable       : {sum(r[2] == 'unreachable' for r in cited)}")
print(f"uncited claims    : {sum(r[2] == 'uncited' for r in rows)}")

Read the output as an eval designer. The number check correctly rejects the forecast (the source never says $50). The India claim shares most words with the source, so a lexical rule cannot decide; it is escalated, and an LLM judge would mark it contradicted (the source says China). This is exactly why cascades have a "not sure" exit instead of forcing a verdict. The dead link is kept out of the accuracy denominator and reported separately, and the uncited sentence is counted for a DEER-style "needs a citation?" check.

3. A pairwise expert-comparison protocol

When you need to claim "our agent's deliverables are as good as a professional's", run a GDPval-style study. The protocol matters more than the code:

import random
from collections import defaultdict

def make_packets(tasks, seed=7):
    """tasks: dicts with task_id, prompt, ai_file, human_file.
    Randomize which deliverable is A or B; keep the key away from raters."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    packets, key = [], {}
    for t in tasks:
        ai_first = rng.random() < 0.5
        a, b = (t["ai_file"], t["human_file"]) if ai_first else (t["human_file"], t["ai_file"])
        pid = f"P{len(packets):04d}"
        packets.append({"packet": pid, "prompt": t["prompt"], "A": a, "B": b})
        key[pid] = {"task_id": t["task_id"], "ai_is": "A" if ai_first else "B"}
    return packets, key

def unblind(ratings, key):
    """ratings: (packet, rater, choice) with choice in {"A", "B", "tie"}.
    Returns outcomes from the AI's point of view, grouped by task."""
    by_task = defaultdict(list)
    for pid, rater, choice in ratings:
        k = key[pid]
        out = "tie" if choice == "tie" else ("win" if choice == k["ai_is"] else "loss")
        by_task[k["task_id"]].append(out)
    return by_task

def win_or_tie(by_task):
    outs = [o for v in by_task.values() for o in v]
    return sum(o != "loss" for o in outs) / len(outs)

def cluster_bootstrap(by_task, n_boot=5000, seed=0):
    """Resample TASKS with replacement: ratings of one task are correlated."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    ids = list(by_task)
    stats = sorted(
        win_or_tie({i: by_task[rng.choice(ids)] for i in range(len(ids))})
        for _ in range(n_boot))
    return stats[int(0.025 * n_boot)], stats[int(0.975 * n_boot)]

# With 40 tasks x 3 raters of simulated data (illustrative), this prints:
# win-or-tie rate 0.41  95% CI [0.32, 0.50]

Protocol details that decide whether anyone should believe the result:

  • Blind properly. Convert both deliverables to the same format, remove file metadata, and strip tell-tale phrases ("As an AI..."). If raters can guess which is the AI, the study measures their attitude to AI.
  • Same brief, same inputs, same time budget where that is meaningful, and record the human's time and cost so you can report cost per acceptable deliverable, not just quality.
  • Require a reason with each choice, from a fixed list plus free text ("factual error", "missed requirement", "formatting", "better analysis"). Reasons turn a single number into an error analysis.
  • Measure agreement. Compute how often raters agree (and Cohen's or Fleiss' kappa, Module 13). Low agreement means the task or the question is underspecified, not that the AI is "about human level".
  • Pre-register the metric (win-or-tie, or win-only) before unblinding, and report ties separately.

Expert nuance and pitfalls

Length gaming
Longer reports cover more rubric items and nuggets by accident. Add a length-normalized view, negative items for padding, and compare at similar lengths.
Citation laundering
The agent writes from memory and then attaches a plausible source. Faithfulness checks catch it only if they read the passage, not the title.
Judge cannot see what the agent saw
Paywalls, bot blocks and personalized pages mean the judge may fetch a different page. Log the agent's retrieved text and verify against that snapshot as well as the live page.
Live-web drift
Scores on live benchmarks move as the web changes. Date-stamp every run, rerun baselines on the same day, and keep a frozen-corpus benchmark for regression tests.
Reference contamination
Public reference reports and gold deliverables can leak into training data. Keep a private held-out set (Module 18).
Harness dependence
A real-work score is the model plus the harness plus the tools (Module 21). GDPval-AA and vendor numbers can differ for this reason alone.
One-shot vs iterative
Most benchmarks grade a single attempt with no feedback. Real work involves revision. If your product iterates with the user, evaluate the loop, for example with a simulated client who requests changes.
Cost belongs in the headline
Report tokens, time and dollars per report next to quality. Anthropic found token usage explained most of its research system's BrowseComp variance (Module 11).

Key takeaways

  1. Long, open-ended outputs need layered grading. Automated checks where possible, rubrics for everyday iteration, expert comparisons for milestones.
  2. Separate report quality from citation quality. DeepResearch Bench does this with RACE (reference-relative, adaptive criteria) and FACT (citation accuracy plus effective citations). Report a precision-like and a volume-like citation metric together.
  3. Faithfulness is not factuality. A citation checker cannot catch a claim faithfully copied from a wrong source, and FACT-style checks never see uncited claims; DEER-style document-level fact-checking closes that gap.
  4. The citation pipeline is claim, source, passage, verdict, with separate buckets for uncited and unreachable. Control its cost with passage retrieval, cascades, section scoping and sampling, and validate every cheaper stage.
  5. Real-work benchmarks grade in three ways: expert pairwise against human deliverables (GDPval, RLI), rubrics (APEX, ResearchRubrics), or state checks in simulated environments (TheAgentCompany, Toolathlon).
  6. Know what the headline means. Win-or-tie rate, automation rate and task success answer different questions, none of them is "jobs replaced", and small task counts give wide intervals.
  7. Build your own with the same parts: a rubric with mandatory and negative items, a claim checker with an "escalate" exit, and a blinded, randomized, multi-rater pairwise study bootstrapped by task.

Check yourself

1. In RACE, a target report's weighted score is 6.0 and the reference report's is 6.0. What is the relative score, and what does it mean?

RACE reports target ÷ (target + reference). Equal scores give 0.5, meaning parity with the reference, which is why the quality of the reference matters so much.

2. Agent A has 98% citation accuracy with 4 effective citations per report. Agent B has 85% accuracy with 60 effective citations. Which statement is right?

Accuracy is precision-like and effective citations is volume-like. Each is easy to game alone, which is why FACT reports both, and why citation coverage (claims needing support that have it) is worth adding.

3. Your citation checker marks every claim in a report as supported, yet an expert finds three false statements. Which explanation fits best?

This is the bottom-left cell of the faithfulness vs factuality grid, plus the blind spot for uncited claims. Add document-level fact-checking and source-quality scoring.

4. In TheAgentCompany, an agent passes 4 of 8 checkpoints on a task. What is its partial-completion score?

0.5 × 4/8 + 0.5 × 0 = 0.25. Half of the credit is reserved for full completion, so half the work earns a quarter of the score.

5. A headline says "AI can now automate 2.5% of remote jobs", citing the Remote Labor Index launch result. What is the most accurate correction?

Automation rate is per project, on a specific sample, one attempt, judged against a client-acceptance bar. Six successes out of 240 gives a 95% interval of roughly 1.2% to 5.3%.

6. Your judge budget for verifying citations is ten times the agent's cost. Which change keeps the most signal for the least money?

Passage retrieval, cascades and stratified sampling cut cost by large factors while keeping the verdicts that matter; a cheaper judge without validation just gives a cheaper wrong answer.
Expert corner: interview questions
  1. "Design an eval for our deep-research product." Collect 30 to 50 real queries stratified by type. Per task: an expert rubric with mandatory and negative items, a reference report for relative scoring, and a nugget list for coverage. Run a claim-level citation pipeline (extract, fetch, passage, cascade to a judge) reporting citation accuracy, citation coverage, unreachable rate and effective citations, plus a document-level check of uncited claims. Add time-sensitive probes for recency and questions with no clean answer for calibration. Run a frozen-corpus set (BrowseComp-Plus style) for regressions and a live-web set for realism, date-stamped. Validate every judge against expert labels. Report cost per report next to quality.
  2. "How does RACE differ from asking a judge for a 1-to-10 score?" It adapts dimension weights and criteria to each task, scores against concrete criteria rather than a vibe, and anchors the score to a reference report judged in the same call, which cancels much of the judge's absolute-scale drift. The cost is needing a good reference per task, and scores that are relative to it.
  3. "What does FACT miss, and how would you fix it?" It checks only claims that carry a citation, and it checks support rather than truth. Add a "does this sentence need a citation?" judge and search-based verification of uncited claims (DEER-style), source-quality scoring, and factuality checks against trusted references for key numbers.
  4. "Compare GDPval and the Remote Labor Index." Both use real professional deliverables as the bar and human experts as graders. GDPval asks experts which of two deliverables is better (win-or-tie rate against a professional), on tasks written by occupational experts. RLI asks whether the AI deliverable would be accepted by a client as the paid project (automation rate), on real freelance projects, and adds Elo between agents. GDPval measures closeness to expert quality; RLI measures the rate of end-to-end acceptable work.
  5. "When would you build a simulated environment like TheAgentCompany instead of using expert graders?" When the work happens in systems whose state you can inspect, when you need to rerun on every change, and when correctness is mostly objective (right ticket closed, right file, right numbers). Keep expert or rubric grading for the parts that are about quality and judgment, and seed the environment with realistic data so tasks are not trivially easy.
  6. "Your pairwise study shows 52% win-or-tie against professionals. Can you claim parity?" Only with the interval, computed by bootstrapping over tasks; with 40 tasks it could easily span the low 40s to the low 60s. Also check rater agreement, whether blinding held (could raters tell?), whether ties dominate, how tasks were sampled, and whether the result holds per occupation rather than only on average.
