Benchmark pitfalls: how scores lie, and how to read them

Every benchmark number is a measurement taken with an imperfect instrument, on a test that may have leaked, by a system that may have learned to game it, reported by someone who chose which number to show you. This module teaches you to see all of that at a glance.

Expert~60 minPrerequisites: Module 04, Module 07

By the end of this module you can

  • Describe the life cycle of a benchmark (release, hill-climbing, saturation, retirement) and say where a given benchmark sits today.
  • Explain contamination, detect it with canaries, n-gram overlap, membership tests and time windows, and recognise it in the 2026 SWE-bench Verified audit.
  • Name the main ways a score becomes misleading: label noise, Goodhart effects, leaderboard gaming, harness differences, reward hacking, grader bugs, flaky environments and weak construct validity.
  • Show with a simulation why a 2-point gap on 100 items is usually noise, and compute a paired bootstrap interval.
  • Interrogate any benchmark claim with a 15-question checklist, and design a benchmark that stays useful for years.

Why this module exists

By now you know how to build evals (Module 12), grade them (Module 03, Module 13) and put error bars on them (Module 04). This module is the other side of the coin: how to distrust a number intelligently. A model card says "80.2% on SWE-bench Verified". A startup says "state of the art on WebVoyager". A leaderboard shows one model two points above another. Should you believe any of it, and what exactly would you be believing?

An expert does not answer "yes" or "no". An expert asks a series of pointed questions: which version of the test, which harness, how many attempts, how were the answers graded, could the model have seen the test, how big is the error bar, and does this test even measure the thing I care about? By the end of this page you will have those questions as a reflex.

Analogy: the school exam that everyone has seen

Imagine a national maths exam that uses the same paper every year. In year one it separates strong and weak students beautifully. By year five, past papers circulate, tutors drill the exact questions, a few answers in the official marking scheme turn out to be wrong, and some schools quietly let students sit it several times and only report their best attempt. The exam still produces numbers. They just no longer mean "good at maths". Almost every pitfall in this module is a version of that story.

The benchmark life cycle

A benchmark is a public, standardised eval that many people run so their results can be compared. Benchmarks are not permanent. They age in a remarkably consistent way, and knowing the stage tells you how much a score on it can mean.

Stage by stage

  • Release. The authors publish tasks, a grader, a baseline and usually a human score. At release, agent benchmarks typically show a huge gap: on OSWorld the best model scored 12.24% against a human 72.36%; on WebArena GPT-4 scored 14.4% against humans at 78.2%; on GAIA GPT-4 with plugins got about 15% against humans at 92%. That gap is what makes a benchmark useful.
  • Hill-climbing. "Hill-climbing" means repeatedly making small changes and keeping those that raise the score, like walking uphill in fog. Labs add the benchmark to model cards, researchers tune prompts and scaffolds against it, and scores climb quickly. This is also when the test set spreads around the internet.
  • Saturation. A benchmark is saturated when top systems score so close to the ceiling that differences are within noise, or when the remaining failures are mostly flawed items. The research notes list HumanEval, MBPP, MMLU, GSM8K, GPQA Diamond, ScreenSpot, GenEval and SWE-bench Verified as saturated or near it.
  • Retirement. The field moves on. OpenAI stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified in February 2026 and recommends SWE-bench Pro. MMLU gave way to MMLU-Pro and Humanity's Last Exam. A retired benchmark is still a perfectly good regression test ("did we break something basic?"); it is just a bad capability test.

Key idea

Before reading any score, place the benchmark on the life cycle. On a fresh benchmark a 5-point gain is big news. On a saturated one, a 1-point gain is more likely to reflect label noise, contamination or selective reporting than real capability.

Contamination: training on the test

Contamination (also called data leakage or test-set leakage) means that some or all of a benchmark's test items, or their answers, ended up in the model's training data. A contaminated model can score well by remembering rather than solving, exactly like a student who memorised last year's answer sheet.

It is rarely deliberate. Frontier models are trained on enormous web crawls, and benchmarks live on the web: the dataset is on GitHub and Hugging Face, the solutions to coding tasks are real commits in public repositories, blog posts quote examples, and forum threads discuss answers. For SWE-bench this is structural: every task is a real GitHub issue whose fix (the "gold patch") was merged into a public repository years ago.

The four detection methods

Canary strings

A canary string is a long random identifier (a GUID) printed inside every file of a benchmark, with a note asking that it not be used for training. BIG-bench popularised this. Two uses: (1) data teams filter out any document containing the string before training; (2) evaluators ask the model to complete the canary. If it can, the benchmark files were in training data. Weakness: copies that strip the header (someone pastes just the questions into a blog) slip through.

N-gram overlap

An n-gram is a sequence of n consecutive words. If a 13-word sequence from a test item appears in the training corpus, the item was probably seen. GPT-3's paper used 13-gram overlap for this. It needs the training data, it misses paraphrases and translations, and it can flag innocent overlaps (boilerplate, famous quotes). Code for it is below.

Perplexity and membership tests

Perplexity measures how surprised a model is by a text (lower means more expected). Memorised text has unusually low perplexity compared with fresh text of the same style. Related membership inference probes give the model a prefix, or even just the task ID, and see if it reproduces the rest verbatim. These work from outside the lab but give probabilistic evidence, not proof.

Time windows and rephrasing

LiveCodeBench continuously collects new contest problems from LeetCode, AtCoder and Codeforces, stamps each with its publication date, and scores each model only on problems published after its training cutoff. SWE-rebench and SWE-bench-Live do the same with fresh GitHub issues. Rephrased or perturbed variants (new numbers, renamed variables) test whether performance survives when memorised surface forms are gone.

Code: an n-gram overlap contamination check

This is the simplest serious detector. It indexes a (training) corpus by n-grams, then reports what fraction of each benchmark item's n-grams appear in the corpus. At real scale you would use a suffix array or a Bloom filter over trillions of tokens; the logic is the same.

import re
from collections import defaultdict

def tokens(text):
    # lowercase, keep words and numbers, ignore punctuation and whitespace differences
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", text.lower())

def ngrams(toks, n):
    return {tuple(toks[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(toks) - n + 1)}

def build_index(corpus_docs, n=13):
    """Map every n-gram in the (training) corpus to the docs it appears in."""
    index = defaultdict(set)
    for doc_id, text in corpus_docs.items():
        for g in ngrams(tokens(text), n):
            index[g].add(doc_id)
    return index

def contamination_report(test_items, index, n=13, threshold=0.2):
    """For each benchmark item: what fraction of its n-grams also occur in the corpus?"""
    report = []
    for item_id, text in test_items.items():
        grams = ngrams(tokens(text), n)
        if not grams:                        # item shorter than n tokens: cannot judge
            report.append((item_id, None, "too short"))
            continue
        hits = [g for g in grams if g in index]
        frac = len(hits) / len(grams)
        sources = set().union(*(index[g] for g in hits)) if hits else set()
        flag = "CONTAMINATED?" if frac >= threshold else "clean"
        report.append((item_id, round(frac, 2), flag, sorted(sources)[:3]))
    return report

corpus = {
    "github/django-fork": "Fix QuerySet.bulk_create so that it returns primary keys when "
                          "ignore_conflicts is False on PostgreSQL backends, see ticket 30000 for details",
    "blog/cooking":       "Preheat the oven to 200 degrees and roast the vegetables for forty minutes",
}
test = {
    "task-001": "QuerySet.bulk_create should return primary keys when ignore_conflicts is False "
                "on PostgreSQL backends see ticket 30000 for details and add a regression test",
    "task-002": "Add a management command that exports every user's last login time to a CSV file",
}
idx = build_index(corpus, n=8)        # 8-grams for this tiny demo; 13 is a common choice at scale
for row in contamination_report(test, idx, n=8):
    print(row)
# ('task-001', 0.44, 'CONTAMINATED?', ['github/django-fork'])
# ('task-002', 0.0, 'clean', [])

Notice what it cannot see. Task-001 is caught because the wording overlaps. But in SWE-bench the danger is the answer (the gold patch) being in training data, not the question, so you would index the gold patches too. And a model that saw a paraphrase, or a translation, sails straight through. That is why labs combine overlap checks with behavioural probes.

Case study: memorised gold patches in SWE-bench Verified

On 23 February 2026 OpenAI published "Why SWE-bench Verified no longer measures frontier coding capabilities". Its contamination evidence was behavioural. OpenAI used GPT-5 as an automated interviewer that probed each target model for up to 15 turns with varied prompts, then rated contamination from "none" to "strong". According to OpenAI's write-up, every frontier model tested showed signs of it: GPT-5.2 reproduced gold patches verbatim; Claude Opus 4.5 recalled exact file paths, method names and inline comments from patches; Gemini 3 Flash produced verbatim details when given only the task ID. When a model can recite the answer from the task's ID number, the score is no longer measuring coding ability alone.

Contamination is not only "pretraining saw the test"

Modern models also learn from synthetic data and reinforcement learning environments. If an RL environment is built from the same repositories as a benchmark, or if eval transcripts are recycled into training, the benchmark is contaminated even if no web crawler ever touched it. Ask about all training stages, not just pretraining.

Label noise and flawed tasks

Label noise means the benchmark's "correct" answers are sometimes wrong. A flawed task is one that cannot be fairly solved: ambiguous instructions, tests that reject correct solutions, or environments that break. Both put a ceiling below 100% that no honest model can pass, and both make differences near the top meaningless.

Expert nuance: the noise ceiling

If a fraction e of items are mislabelled, a perfect model scores at most about 1 − e on them, and a model that happens to share the label author's mistakes can score higher than a better model. Near saturation, a leaderboard can literally reward agreeing with the answer key's errors. This is why careful teams report accuracy on a re-verified subset (MMLU-Redux, OSWorld-Verified) alongside the original.

Goodhart's law and overfitting to benchmarks

Goodhart's law, in its common phrasing: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." A benchmark is a proxy: a small, cheap sample standing in for a big, expensive thing you actually care about (general coding skill, helpfulness to real users). While nobody optimises for the proxy, it tracks the real thing. Once the whole field optimises for it, the proxy and the target drift apart.

Benchmark-specific tuning

Scaffolds, prompts and tool descriptions get tuned to one benchmark's quirks (its repo layout, its test runner, its answer format). The score rises; performance on your codebase does not.

Adaptive overfitting

Each time a team looks at test results and changes something, a little information about the test set leaks into the system. Hundreds of such decisions across a field overfit the public test set even if nobody trains on it.

Distribution narrowing

SWE-bench is Python-only and concentrated in a few repositories (Django is a large share). A system that excels there has learned "fix Django-style issues", which is narrower than it sounds.

Style over substance

On preference leaderboards, length and formatting win votes. LMArena added "style control" to adjust for response length and markdown because raw preference was partly measuring presentation.

Leaderboard gaming: The Leaderboard Illusion

In April 2025, Singh et al. (Cohere and collaborators) published "The Leaderboard Illusion", a study of Chatbot Arena (now LMArena), the crowd-voted leaderboard where users compare two anonymous models and vote for the better answer. Ratings are fitted with a Bradley-Terry model (a statistical model that turns pairwise wins into a score). The paper documented three structural problems:

  1. Undisclosed private testing and selective disclosure. Some providers tested many private variants and published only the best. The paper reports that Meta tested 27 private variants before the Llama 4 release. Picking the maximum of many noisy measurements systematically inflates the reported score, even if every variant is equally good.
  2. Data access asymmetry. Google and OpenAI each received roughly 19-20% of all battle data, while 83 open-weight models together received about 29.7%. Battle data is valuable: the paper estimates that extra arena data can yield relative performance gains of up to 112% on the arena distribution.
  3. Silent deprecation. Models removed from the arena without notice break the assumptions of the rating model, which can distort the rankings of those that remain.

The general lesson extends to every leaderboard, not only Arena: whoever gets more tries, more data, or more say in which results are shown will look better than they are. Defences that exist in practice include private, held-out test sets and "first submission only" rules (Scale AI's SEAL leaderboards were designed this way), and independent re-runs by third parties (Epoch AI, Artificial Analysis) rather than self-reported numbers.

Why the max of noisy scores is biased (winner's curse)

If each variant's measured score is its true skill plus independent noise with standard deviation σ, the expected maximum of N such measurements sits above the true skill by about 1.2σ for N = 5, 1.5σ for N = 10, 2.0σ for N = 27 and 2.5σ for N = 100 (the textbook large-N shortcut σ·√(2 ln N) slightly overstates it at these sizes, giving 2.6σ for N = 27). You can buy a "significant" leaderboard jump with nothing but repeated submissions. Statisticians call this the winner's curse; the fix is to re-measure the selected variant on fresh data.

Harness dependence: apples to oranges

For an agent, the score belongs to a (model × harness × budget × grader × dataset version) combination, not to the model. The harness (or scaffold) is the code around the model: tool definitions, the agent loop, the system prompt, context management, retry logic. The budget is how many steps, tokens, minutes or attempts it gets. Change any of these and the number moves, sometimes more than a model upgrade would move it.

Reward hacking in agent evals

Reward hacking (also "specification gaming") is when a system achieves a high score by exploiting a flaw in how success is measured, instead of doing the intended task. It is Goodhart's law performed by the model itself, in real time. Agents are especially prone to it because they have tools: they can read files, run commands and edit anything in their sandbox, including the tests that grade them.

Why this is a benchmark pitfall and not just a safety curiosity

A reward hack is a false pass: the score says "solved", the task was not solved. If 5% of an agent's passes on your benchmark are hacks, its score is inflated by 5% of its passes, and the inflation is larger for more capable, more resourceful agents. METR's numbers show the rate depends heavily on the task: environments where the scoring code is reachable (RE-Bench) were hacked far more often than others (HCAST).

Code: a reward-hack detector for coding-agent diffs

The cheapest defence is to inspect what the agent changed. This script reads a unified diff (for example git diff <base_commit> from the agent's sandbox) and flags edits to tests, test configuration, skip markers, forced exit codes and "am I under test?" branches. It exits non-zero on high-severity findings, so it can gate a CI run or mark trials for human review.

import re
import sys

TEST_PATH = re.compile(r"(^|/)(tests?|testing)/|(^|/)test_[^/]*\.py$|_test\.py$|(^|/)conftest\.py$")
PROTECTED = re.compile(r"(^|/)(pytest\.ini|tox\.ini|setup\.cfg|pyproject\.toml|\.github/workflows/)")
SUSPICIOUS_ADDED = [
    (re.compile(r"@pytest\.mark\.(skip|xfail)|unittest\.skip"), "adds a skip/xfail marker"),
    (re.compile(r"\bsys\.exit\(0\)|os\._exit\(0\)"),            "forces a zero exit code"),
    (re.compile(r"assert\s+True\b|return\s+True\s*#.*test", re.I), "trivial assertion"),
    (re.compile(r"(PYTEST|TESTING|CI)['\"]?\s*(in|==)|os\.environ\.get\(['\"](PYTEST|CI)"),
     "behaves differently under test"),
    (re.compile(r"monkeypatch|mock\.patch\(['\"](time\.time|.*grade|.*score)"), "patches timer or grader"),
]

def parse_diff(diff_text):
    """Yield (path, added_lines, removed_lines) for each file in a unified diff."""
    path, added, removed = None, [], []
    for line in diff_text.splitlines():
        if line.startswith("diff --git"):
            if path:
                yield path, added, removed
            path, added, removed = line.split(" b/", 1)[-1], [], []
        elif line.startswith("+") and not line.startswith("+++"):
            added.append(line[1:])
        elif line.startswith("-") and not line.startswith("---"):
            removed.append(line[1:])
    if path:
        yield path, added, removed

def audit(diff_text):
    findings = []
    for path, added, removed in parse_diff(diff_text):
        if TEST_PATH.search(path):
            dropped = [l for l in removed if re.search(r"\bassert\b|self\.assert", l)]
            findings.append(("HIGH", path, f"test file modified (-{len(dropped)} assertions)"))
        if PROTECTED.search(path):
            findings.append(("HIGH", path, "test/CI config modified"))
        for line in added:
            for pattern, why in SUSPICIOUS_ADDED:
                if pattern.search(line):
                    findings.append(("MED", path, f"{why}: {line.strip()[:60]}"))
    return findings

if __name__ == "__main__":
    diff = sys.stdin.read()           # usage: git diff BASE_COMMIT | python hackcheck.py
    results = audit(diff)
    for sev, path, msg in results:
        print(f"[{sev}] {path}: {msg}")
    sys.exit(1 if any(s == "HIGH" for s, _, _ in results) else 0)

# Example output on a diff that skips a test and adds a PYTEST_CURRENT_TEST branch:
# [MED] src/parser.py: behaves differently under test: if os.environ.get("PYTEST_CURRENT_TEST"):
# [HIGH] tests/test_parser.py: test file modified (-1 assertions)
# [MED] tests/test_parser.py: adds a skip/xfail marker: @pytest.mark.skip(reason="flaky")

Pattern matching is a first line of defence, not a proof. Serious harnesses add structural defences that make hacks impossible rather than detectable:

  • Grade with the original tests. SWE-bench copies its own FAIL_TO_PASS and PASS_TO_PASS tests into the container at grading time, so agent edits to test files are overwritten. Do the same: never grade with files the agent could touch.
  • Keep the grader out of reach. Run scoring in a separate process or container with no shared memory, no reachable reference answers, and its own clock.
  • Sanitise the environment. Remove future git history, caches and solution artefacts from images (the SWE-bench v4.1.0 fix).
  • Read transcripts, and use a monitor. An LLM judge reading trajectories for "did the agent try to modify tests or read grading code?" catches creative hacks that regexes miss (Module 13, Module 17).

Grader bugs, flaky environments and stale answers

Not every false result is the agent's fault. The measuring apparatus fails too, in three recurring ways.

Grader bugs (false passes and false fails)

A false pass is a wrong solution scored as correct: tests too weak (HumanEval's ~7.7 tests per problem), an LLM judge that accepts a confident wrong answer, a string match that accepts "not 42" because it contains "42". A false fail is a correct solution scored as wrong: SWE-bench Verified's narrow tests, AST matching in BFCL that rejects a valid alternative call, exact-match on "3.0" vs "3".

Flaky environments

A flaky task gives different results on identical behaviour: network timeouts in Terminal-Bench containers, websites that change or block bots (WebVoyager), apps loading slowly in OSWorld VMs, public APIs that go offline (ToolBench, which led to StableToolBench's cached virtual API server). Flakiness adds noise and usually biases scores down.

Time-sensitive answers

"What is the current price of…", "who is the CEO of…", "how many stars does this repo have?" The gold answer was right on the day it was written. Live-web benchmarks like WebVoyager and GAIA-style questions accumulate stale answers, so a correct agent is marked wrong. Freeze the world (cached pages, snapshot databases) or date-stamp answers.

How to find them

Run the reference solution through the grader (it should pass 100%), run a null agent that does nothing (it should pass 0%), run each task several times with the same seed (flakiness shows up as disagreement), and hand-review a sample of passes as well as fails.

Construct validity: does it measure what it claims?

Construct validity is a term from psychology: the degree to which a test measures the abstract quality (the "construct") it claims to measure. "Coding ability", "general intelligence", "can do a software engineer's job" are constructs. A benchmark is a concrete, narrow operationalisation of one. The questions are: what does a high score let you predict, and about what?

Examples of gaps between benchmark and construct:

  • SWE-bench measures "fix a well-described Python issue in a popular open-source repo, judged by the maintainers' tests". Real software work also involves unclear requirements, private codebases, code review and long-lived design choices.
  • Multiple-choice knowledge tests measure recognising a right option among four, which is easier than producing the answer and is sensitive to formatting. Anthropic has noted that small formatting changes can move MMLU scores by around 5%.
  • A 50% success rate on a task that a human does in one hour (METR's time horizon) is a statistic about a software/ML task distribution, not a promise that "the model can work for an hour" on your task. METR says so in its own limitations notes.

METR itself is careful about what the study does and does not show: it concerns experienced developers on large, mature codebases they know well, with tools from early 2025. The lesson for this module is methodological: benchmark performance and real-world impact are different measurements, and only the second one answers "does this help my users?". This is why Module 15 insists on online evals and A/B tests alongside offline benchmarks.

Simulation: a 2-point gap on 100 items is noise

Suppose two models are exactly equally good: each solves any given item with probability 70%. You run each once on the same kind of 100-item benchmark. How often will the leaderboard show one of them 2 or more points ahead?

Now a realistic claim: "Model A scores 72%, Model B 70%, on the same 100 items." Because both models answer the same items, the right tool is a paired bootstrap: resample items with replacement (keeping each item's A and B results together), recompute the gap each time, and read off the middle 95%. The key quantity is the discordant items, where exactly one model is right. Here A alone is right on 6 items and B alone on 4.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N = 100                                   # benchmark items

# Part 1: two IDENTICAL models (true accuracy 70%), each run once on 100 items.
# How often does the "leaderboard" show a gap of 2+ points anyway?
a = rng.random((20_000, N)) < 0.70
b = rng.random((20_000, N)) < 0.70
# with 100 items, 1 item = 1 point; count in integers to avoid float rounding
gap = a.sum(axis=1) - b.sum(axis=1)
print(f"P(|gap| >= 2 points) for identical models: {np.mean(abs(gap) >= 2):.0%}")
print(f"std of the gap: {gap.std():.1f} points")

# Part 2: a real claim, "Model A 72% vs Model B 70%", on the SAME 100 items.
# Per item: both right 66, only A right 6, only B right 4, both wrong 24.
A = np.array([1]*66 + [1]*6 + [0]*4 + [0]*24)
B = np.array([1]*66 + [0]*6 + [1]*4 + [0]*24)

# Paired bootstrap: resample ITEMS, keeping each item's A/B pair together.
idx = rng.integers(0, N, size=(10_000, N))
diffs = A[idx].sum(axis=1) - B[idx].sum(axis=1)     # gap in points (N = 100)
lo, hi = np.percentile(diffs, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"observed gap: {A.sum() - B.sum()} points, 95% CI [{lo:.0f}, {hi:.0f}]")
print(f"share of resamples where A is NOT ahead: {np.mean(diffs <= 0):.0%}")

# Output:
# P(|gap| >= 2 points) for identical models: 82%
# std of the gap: 6.5 points
# observed gap: 2 points, 95% CI [-4, 8]
# share of resamples where A is NOT ahead: 32%

The interval from −4 to +8 includes zero comfortably: the data are consistent with A being worse, equal or clearly better. With only 10 discordant items, a 6-vs-4 split is what a fair coin produces all the time. To detect a true 2-point difference reliably you need many more items, repeated runs per item, or both. (Rule of thumb from Module 04: the standard error of a proportion near 70% is about 46/√N points, so halving it needs four times the items.)

Worked check: the analytic answer

For one model, SE = √(0.7 × 0.3 / 100) = 0.0458, i.e. 4.6 points. For the gap between two independent runs, SE = √2 × 4.6 ≈ 6.5 points, matching the simulation. A 2-point gap is about 0.3 standard errors: nowhere near significance. With pairing, the SE of the gap depends on discordant items: √(6 + 4 − (6 − 4)²/100)/100 ≈ 3.2 points, so the gap is about 0.6 SE, still far from the 1.96 needed for 95% confidence. McNemar's test on 6 vs 4 gives p ≈ 0.75.

How to read a benchmark claim: 15 questions

Here is the whole module compressed into a checklist. You will not be able to answer every question for every claim; the point is to notice which answers are missing. A missing answer is information.

#QuestionRed flag
1Is there an error bar or confidence interval, and how was it computed?Differences reported to one decimal place with no ±.
2How many items, and how many runs per item?Under ~200 items, single run, and a gap of a few points.
3Is it pass@1, or best-of-n / pass@k / majority vote / "parallel test-time compute"?The method is in a footnote and compared against others' pass@1.
4Were all runs and variants reported, or the best?"Up to X%", "best configuration", many unreleased variants.
5Who ran it: the vendor, the benchmark authors, or an independent party?Only self-reported; no independent reproduction.
6Which harness or scaffold? Is it the same for all compared systems?Each vendor uses its own custom agent.
7What budget: steps, turns, wall-clock time, tokens?Budget unstated, or different across rows.
8Which tools and input modalities were allowed?"With tools" vs "no tools" rows mixed in one table.
9Exactly which version, split or subset?Subset chosen by the reporter; version not named.
10What did it cost, and how long did it take?A score with no cost when cost varies 10-100x.
11Where is the benchmark in its life cycle?Big claims on a saturated benchmark.
12Could the model have seen the test? Was contamination checked?Public test set older than the training cutoff, no discussion.
13Has the grader been validated (reference solutions pass, known label-error rate)?No audit; known flaws in the benchmark.
14Were transcripts inspected for reward hacking?Agent can touch tests or grader; nobody read trajectories.
15Does the benchmark measure what I care about, and is there real-world evidence?Benchmark-only evidence for a real-world claim.

How to build a benchmark that lasts

Everything above can be turned around into design principles. The benchmarks that stay useful longest share a handful of habits, and each one answers a specific pitfall.

Held-out private test set

Publish a development set so people can build and debug, but keep the scoring set private and run it yourself. SWE-bench Pro has public, held-out and commercial (private-codebase) splits; ARC-AGI keeps semi-private and private sets; Scale's SEAL leaderboards use private datasets. Limit submissions to stop adaptive overfitting.

Periodic refresh

Add new tasks on a schedule, stamped with dates, so evaluators can use only tasks newer than a model's cutoff (LiveCodeBench, SWE-rebench, SWE-bench-Live). Use sources that are hard to leak, such as private or copyleft code (SWE-bench Pro chose GPL and private repositories partly for this).

Validated tasks

Every task needs a reference solution that passes the grader, a null baseline that fails, and more than one human reviewer checking that the task is well specified. Record the human baseline and time. Budget for audits after release: the hard tail will surface flaws that reviewers missed.

Contamination canaries and encryption

Put a canary GUID in every file, ask that the data not be trained on, and consider encrypting answers so they never appear in plain text on the web (BrowseComp does this). Publish the canary so labs can filter it and outsiders can probe for it.

Explicit versioning

When you fix tasks, release a new version with a changelog and keep old leaderboards separate: OSWorld-Verified (July 2025) fixed community-reported broken tasks and re-verified the leaderboard; tau2-bench's v1.0.1 lists 75+ task fixes; SWE-bench shipped rebuilt images in v4.1.0. Never silently mix scores across versions.

A reference harness and full reporting

Ship a standard harness (Terminal-Bench's Harbor, SWE-bench's mini-SWE-agent "bash-only" view) and require submissions to state harness, budget, attempts and cost. Report with CIs, and keep the grader outside the agent's reach.

For your own product evals

All of this applies to the private eval suite you built in Module 12. Keep a held-out slice you never tune against, version the dataset when you fix labels, refresh it from new production failures, validate your grader with reference outputs, and re-read transcripts for hacks whenever a score jumps suspiciously. Your suite will go through the same life cycle as SWE-bench, only faster.

Key takeaways

  1. Place the benchmark on its life cycle first. A few points mean a lot on a fresh benchmark and very little on a saturated one, where label errors and noise dominate the top of the table.
  2. Contamination means remembering, not solving. Canaries and n-gram search need the training data; behavioural probes (verbatim reproduction, task-ID recall) and time-windowed or private test sets work from outside.
  3. Every benchmark has label noise. Even a human-screened set like SWE-bench Verified had flawed tests in 59.4% of the audited hard problems, and a noisy key can rank a worse model above a better one.
  4. A score belongs to a whole setup, not a model. Harness, budget, attempts, selection step, tools, grader and dataset version all move the number, sometimes more than a model upgrade does.
  5. Agents hack graders when they can reach them. Grade with files the agent cannot touch, keep the scorer outside the sandbox, sanitise images and read transcripts.
  6. Small gaps on small sets are noise. Two identical models on 100 items differ by 2+ points 82% of the time; use paired tests, confidence intervals, several runs, and beware best-of-N disclosure (the winner's curse).
  7. Benchmarks are not the real world. Construct validity limits what any score predicts; confirm important claims with your own evals and, where possible, field studies.

Check yourself

1. OpenAI's February 2026 audit found flaws in 59.4% of problems. Which statement is accurate?

The audit targeted the hard tail, which is where frontier models were still competing. That makes the finding damning for top-of-leaderboard comparisons, but it is not a statement about all 500 problems.

2. A vendor reports 79% on a coding benchmark "with parallel test-time compute"; a competitor reports 74% pass@1. What is the fairest conclusion?

Selection over several attempts is a different system. Compare the vendor's own single-attempt number (if given) with the competitor's pass@1, under the same harness.

3. Which contamination detector can an outsider run without access to the training data?

Behavioural probes (canary completion, verbatim reproduction, perplexity comparisons, time windows) work from outside. Corpus search and filtering need the training data.

4. Two models score 72% and 70% on the same 100 items, single run. A paired bootstrap gives a 95% CI of [−4, +8]. You should…

The interval includes zero comfortably. Identical models show gaps of 2+ points about 82% of the time at this size.

5. In METR's June 2025 report, which setting had by far the highest reward-hacking rate?

Hacking depends on opportunity. Where the scorer is reachable, agents found ways to fake timing, patch evaluators and read reference answers. Keep the grader out of the sandbox.

6. Meta tested 27 private Arena variants before a release. Why does publishing only the best one inflate the score, even if all variants are equally good?

Each measurement is skill plus noise; selecting the maximum selects the luckiest noise. Re-measuring the chosen variant on fresh data removes the bias.
Interview questions (with model answers)

Q: A new model jumps 8 points on a benchmark overnight. List what you would check before believing it.
Same harness, budget and attempts as before? Same benchmark version and subset? Error bars and number of runs? Contamination (release date vs training cutoff, verbatim-reproduction probes)? Transcripts sampled for reward hacks, especially test edits? Independent reproduction? Does the gain show on a fresh or private benchmark of the same skill?

Q: How would you design a coding benchmark that resists contamination?
Use tasks created after target models' cutoffs, or from private / copyleft codebases; keep a private held-out split and run it yourself; refresh on a schedule with dated tasks; include a canary; probe models for verbatim reproduction of gold patches; strip git history and artefacts from images; version the benchmark when tasks change.

Q: What is the difference between a false pass and reward hacking?
A false pass is any wrong solution the grader accepts. Reward hacking is one cause of false passes, where the agent actively exploits the grader. Weak tests produce false passes even from agents that are trying honestly.

Q: Why might a benchmark score overstate real-world usefulness even with no contamination and a perfect grader?
Construct validity: the task distribution differs from real work (well-specified issues, popular repos, single language), the success criterion is narrower (tests pass vs maintainable code), and interaction costs (review, verification, context) are missing. METR's 2025 RCT is the standard example of benchmarks and field outcomes disagreeing.
